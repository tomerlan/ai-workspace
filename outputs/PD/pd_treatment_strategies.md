# Parkinson's Disease: Treatment Strategies

---

## The Treatment Landscape

PD treatment currently divides cleanly into two categories with very different levels of evidence. **Symptomatic treatments** — chiefly dopamine replacement — are highly effective at managing motor signs for years to decades, but do not slow the underlying neurodegeneration. **Disease-modifying treatments** — aimed at slowing, halting, or reversing cell loss — remain an unmet clinical goal: no agent has yet demonstrated convincing neuroprotection in a phase 3 trial, though several candidates are in late-stage development.

The sections below are ordered within each category from most effective/established to most speculative.

---

## Symptomatic Treatments

---

### Levodopa/Carbidopa — Dopamine Precursor Replacement
*The most effective symptomatic treatment in all of medicine for PD. The reference standard against which all other motor therapies are measured.*

Levodopa crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine by residual nigrostriatal neurons. Carbidopa (a peripheral DOPA decarboxylase inhibitor) is co-administered to prevent peripheral conversion, increasing brain delivery and reducing nausea. The motor response is dramatic in early PD — most patients experience a near-normalization of motor function.

**Limitations.** As neurons are lost over years, the "buffering" capacity of the remaining terminal field shrinks. The therapeutic window narrows: with fewer terminals to store and release dopamine, plasma levodopa fluctuations translate directly to motor fluctuations — ON/OFF cycling, peak-dose dyskinesias (involuntary movements). Standard oral dosing every 3–4 hours becomes increasingly insufficient in mid-to-late disease. Approximately 50% of patients develop motor complications after 5 years of levodopa therapy.

**Formulation variants.** Controlled-release formulations (e.g., Rytary) reduce fluctuations modestly. Levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel (LCIG / Duodopa), delivered by a percutaneous gastrojejunostomy pump, provides continuous intestinal infusion and is highly effective at eliminating motor fluctuations in advanced disease. Inhaled levodopa (Inbrija) provides rescue dosing for sudden OFF states.

---

### Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)
*The most effective intervention for motor fluctuations and dyskinesias in patients who respond to levodopa but develop complications.*

High-frequency electrical stimulation (130–180 Hz) delivered by an implanted electrode into the **subthalamic nucleus (STN)** or **globus pallidus interna (GPi)** suppresses aberrant oscillatory activity in the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuit. In appropriately selected patients (good levodopa response, no significant cognitive impairment, <70 years as a rough heuristic), STN-DBS typically reduces motor fluctuation time by 50–70%, reduces dyskinesias, and can allow a 30–50% reduction in levodopa dose. GPi-DBS has similar motor benefit with less cognitive risk and better dyskinesia control; STN-DBS allows greater medication reduction.

**Limitations.** DBS is not neuroprotective — underlying progression continues. It does not improve non-motor symptoms and may worsen cognition, speech, and gait in susceptible patients. Requires neurosurgery and ongoing programming. Effects on axial symptoms (gait, balance) are inconsistent.

**Emerging variant.** Adaptive DBS (closed-loop) — the stimulator reads a biomarker (local field potential beta oscillations) and adjusts stimulation intensity in real time. Reduces stimulation side effects and improves therapeutic specificity. Not yet widely available.

---

### Dopamine Agonists
*Second in motor efficacy to levodopa; used as monotherapy in early disease or in combination with levodopa.*

Dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine patch) directly stimulate postsynaptic dopamine receptors, bypassing the progressively depleted presynaptic terminal. Their longer half-lives produce smoother dopaminergic stimulation and less motor fluctuation than oral levodopa. Often used as first-line therapy in younger patients to delay levodopa initiation and thus delay motor complications.

**Limitations.** Less effective than levodopa for motor control. Significant side effects: impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping) in 10–20% of patients, daytime somnolence, hallucinations, leg edema. **Apomorphine** — the most potent dopamine agonist — is given subcutaneously as either rescue injections (for sudden OFF) or continuous pump infusion (for advanced disease with refractory fluctuations), providing levodopa-equivalent motor control without the GI absorption problem.

---

### MAO-B Inhibitors
*Mild-to-moderate motor benefit; well-tolerated; can be used at any stage.*

Monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) inhibitors — rasagiline, selegiline, safinamide — block the primary enzyme responsible for dopamine catabolism in the striatum, extending the effect of both endogenous and levodopa-derived dopamine. Early clinical trials (DATATOP, ADAGIO) raised hope for neuroprotection, but the observed benefit was ultimately attributed to symptomatic effects. Safinamide additionally has glutamate-modulating properties, providing mild adjunctive non-dopaminergic benefit.

**Limitations.** Motor effect is modest — approximately 2–3 UPDRS points as monotherapy. Used primarily as add-on to levodopa for wearing off. Diet interactions (tyramine) are minimal with selective MAO-B doses.

---

### COMT Inhibitors
*Adjunctive therapy to extend levodopa duration; reduces wearing off.*

Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) inhibitors — entacapone, opicapone, tolcapone — block the peripheral methylation of levodopa to 3-OMD (3-O-methyldopa), increasing levodopa bioavailability and plasma half-life by approximately 30–50%. Reduce daily OFF time by 1–2 hours in patients with wearing off. Opicapone (once daily) has the most favorable tolerability profile. Tolcapone is more potent but carries a rare risk of hepatotoxicity requiring monitoring.

---

### Amantadine
*Primarily used for levodopa-induced dyskinesia; also mild anti-parkinsonian effect.*

Originally an antiviral, amantadine antagonizes NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) glutamate receptors and has mild dopaminergic effects. Extended-release amantadine (Gocovri, Osmolex) is FDA-approved specifically for dyskinesia — a relatively unique indication, as no other approved agent directly reduces dyskinesias without sacrificing motor control. Effect size on dyskinesia is approximately 20–30% reduction in severity.

**Limitations.** Confusion, hallucinations, livedo reticularis, ankle edema. Withdrawal of amantadine in patients who have used it long-term can precipitate sudden deterioration.

---

### Anticholinergics
*Narrow use: tremor-predominant disease in younger patients without cognitive impairment.*

Trihexyphenidyl and benztropine reduce the relative dominance of cholinergic activity in the striatum when dopamine is depleted. Modestly effective for resting tremor — less so for bradykinesia. Rarely used today due to significant cognitive side effects (memory impairment, confusion, hallucinations) that are poorly tolerated in the older PD population.

---

### Focused Ultrasound (FUS) Thalamotomy
*Highly effective for unilateral tremor; irreversible; no hardware implant.*

Transcranial magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (tcMRgFUS) ablates the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus using convergent ultrasound beams. Produces immediate, sustained tremor suppression (>60–70% reduction) in the contralateral limb. Unlike DBS, there is no implanted hardware, no general anesthesia, and no programming — it is performed awake in an MRI scanner. Currently limited to unilateral treatment (bilateral raises balance/gait risk). Does not address bradykinesia or rigidity. FDA-approved for tremor-dominant PD.

## Disease-Modifying Strategies (Investigational)

No treatment in this category has achieved the primary endpoint of slowing neurodegeneration in a definitive phase 3 trial. Several show signals compelling enough to pursue.

---

### GLP-1 Receptor Agonists
*Strongest emerging signal for disease modification. Epidemiological data and one positive small RCT.*

**Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)** receptor agonists (exenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide) — originally developed for type 2 diabetes and obesity — have generated substantial interest in PD based on:

- Large epidemiological studies showing that GLP-1 agonist use in diabetic populations is associated with significantly lower PD incidence and slower progression.
- A randomized controlled trial of exenatide (Athauda et al., Lancet 2017) showing a sustained ~3-point motor benefit (MDS-UPDRS Part III, OFF) compared to placebo, persisting 12 months after drug discontinuation — an atypical finding for a purely symptomatic drug, consistent with structural modification.
- GLP-1 receptors are expressed in dopaminergic neurons of the SNpc. GLP-1 agonism activates neuroprotective intracellular pathways (PI3K/Akt, BDNF), reduces neuroinflammation, and improves mitochondrial function and autophagy in preclinical models.

**Status.** The EXENATIDE-PD3 trial (larger replication) reported in 2024. Semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) is in an NIH-funded phase 2 trial for PD (SUSTAIN-PD). This is currently the most mechanistically plausible and clinically supported disease-modification candidate.

---

### Iron Chelation — Deferiprone
*Rationale well-grounded in neuromelanin/iron biology; trials to date show target engagement but modest clinical benefit.*

Iron accumulation in the SNpc drives oxidative stress through Fenton chemistry (Fe²⁺ → hydroxyl radical). Deferiprone is an oral iron chelator that crosses the blood-brain barrier and preferentially chelates iron from brain regions with high iron burden. The FAIRPARK-II trial (2019, n=372) showed significant reduction in nigral QSM susceptibility (target engagement) but no significant benefit on motor outcomes at 36 weeks, with possible slight worsening, potentially reflecting iron's dual role as both toxic and necessary for dopamine synthesis. Optimal patient selection (early disease, high baseline iron) and dosing remain under investigation.

---

### Alpha-Synuclein Targeting
*Scientifically compelling; trials so far have been largely neutral or negative.*

Several antibodies targeting misfolded alpha-synuclein (prasinezumab, cinpanemab, BIIB054, BAN0805) have completed or are in phase 2 trials. Results to date have been disappointing — most trials did not meet their primary endpoints. Post-hoc analyses in faster-progressing subgroups (e.g., those with higher baseline motor burden) have suggested some signal, prompting redesigned trials. The fundamental challenge may be that by clinical PD diagnosis, synuclein pathology is already widespread and possibly self-propagating beyond antibody reach. Vaccine approaches targeting synuclein are in early trials.

---

### LRRK2 and GBA-Targeted Therapies
*Precision medicine for mutation carriers; high mechanistic rationale.*

**LRRK2** (Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2) — the most common genetic cause of PD — encodes a kinase whose pathological gain-of-function mutations increase kinase activity and impair lysosomal function. LRRK2 kinase inhibitors (DNL201, DNL151/BIIB122) show target engagement in CSF and are in phase 2/3 trials. **GBA** (glucocerebrosidase) mutations — present in ~5–15% of PD patients and associated with faster progression and greater cognitive impairment — are being targeted by enzyme replacement strategies, small-molecule chaperones (ambroxol, currently in trials), and gene therapy. Neither has yet demonstrated clinical disease modification in a completed trial.

---

### Nilotinib (BCR-Abl Inhibitor, Repurposed)
*Intriguing small-study signals; larger trials did not replicate.*

Nilotinib, a chronic myelogenous leukemia drug, was proposed to activate autophagy and clear alpha-synuclein aggregates through c-Abl kinase inhibition. Phase 1 safety trials in Georgetown showed preliminary biomarker changes in CSF alpha-synuclein and dopamine metabolites. Subsequent phase 2 trials (NILO-PD) did not confirm clinical benefit. Not currently in active late-stage development for PD.

---

## Unconventional and Emerging Approaches

---

### Exercise
*The most evidence-supported non-pharmacological intervention. Possibly the strongest disease-modifying candidate that doesn't require a drug.*

Exercise has stronger symptomatic and potentially neuroprotective evidence in PD than any other lifestyle intervention, and rivals some pharmacological secondaries. High-intensity aerobic exercise (treadmill training, cycling at >80% max heart rate) produces consistent improvements in motor function, gait speed, and quality of life. In the SPARX2 trial (2018), high-intensity treadmill training slowed motor progression on MDS-UPDRS Part III compared to moderate-intensity controls.

The mechanism is plausible and multi-pronged: **BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor)** is released acutely after aerobic exercise and supports dopaminergic neuron survival. Exercise increases striatal dopamine turnover, promotes neuroplasticity in the corticostriatal circuits, reduces neuroinflammation, and improves mitochondrial biogenesis. Animal models consistently show exercise delays SNpc neuron loss. Human epidemiological data shows that lifelong physical activity is associated with lower PD incidence.

**Forms with specific evidence:** high-intensity treadmill, forced-rate cycling (tandem cycling at cadences above voluntary capacity), dance (tango, in particular), boxing-style training (Rock Steady Boxing), and resistance training for axial symptoms. The principle across all is: intensity matters.

---

### Gut Microbiome Interventions
*Strong mechanistic rationale; clinical evidence is early-stage.*

The gut-brain axis has become central to PD biology. Alpha-synuclein aggregation may initiate in enteric neurons (supported by epidemiological data showing reduced PD risk after vagotomy and by the finding of synuclein pathology in colonic biopsies years before motor diagnosis). The gut microbiome composition in PD differs significantly from controls — reduced butyrate-producing bacteria, increased pro-inflammatory taxa. Constipation is one of the earliest prodromal features.

**Fecal microbiome transplant (FMT)** — transferring gut microbial communities from a healthy donor — has shown preliminary signals in small pilot trials in PD (improved constipation, modest motor benefit). The TOPAS trial (Denmark, ongoing) is the first powered RCT of FMT in PD. Probiotic interventions targeting specific taxa are also under investigation. This is a biologically credible and potentially scalable intervention if efficacy is confirmed.

---

### Dietary Approaches
*Observational support for Mediterranean and ketogenic diets; no RCT evidence yet.*

**Mediterranean diet** (high in olive oil, vegetables, fish, polyphenols; low in processed food and red meat) is associated in epidemiological studies with lower PD incidence and slower progression. The operative components may include anti-inflammatory polyphenols (especially from extra-virgin olive oil — oleocanthal, oleacein), omega-3 fatty acids, and microbiome-supporting fiber.

**Ketogenic diet** — which shifts energy metabolism from glucose to ketone bodies — has preclinical evidence in PD models: ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate) reduce oxidative stress, enhance mitochondrial function, and have shown modest benefit in a small human pilot study (Vanitallie et al., 2005). Ketones bypass Complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, whose dysfunction is central to PD pathology. Larger controlled trials do not yet exist.

**Intermittent fasting** — through autophagy induction — is mechanistically attractive (alpha-synuclein clearance requires functional autophagy) but lacks controlled human data in PD.

---

### Photobiomodulation (Near-Infrared / Red Light)
*Biologically plausible; evidence is preliminary and based on small studies.*

Near-infrared (NIR) light at wavelengths of 670–1070 nm penetrates brain tissue and is absorbed by cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondria, enhancing ATP production, reducing reactive oxygen species, and reducing neuroinflammation. In MPTP (1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine)-treated mouse models, transcranial NIR light significantly reduced dopaminergic neuron loss in the SNpc.

Transcranial delivery in humans is limited by skull attenuation. Intranasal delivery (which targets the olfactory bulb and prefrontal cortex through thinner bone) and helmet-based transcranial devices are in small pilot trials. A 2021 open-label trial (Berman et al.) showed modest motor improvements with a commercial NIR helmet. A proof-of-concept RCT is underway in Australia. Too early to assess efficacy.

---

### High-Dose Thiamine (Vitamin B1)
*Anecdotal clinical reports; not validated by controlled trials.*

An Italian neurologist (Dr. Antonio Costantini) reported striking motor improvements in PD patients given high-dose parenteral thiamine (100 mg IM twice weekly), with case series published in peer-reviewed journals. The proposed mechanism involves thiamine-dependent enzymatic steps in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) that are rate-limiting in dopaminergic neuron bioenergetics under conditions of functional thiamine deficiency. No controlled trial has confirmed the effect. The intervention is inexpensive and has a favorable safety profile, which has led to patient-driven adoption despite absent RCT evidence.

---

### Hyperbaric Oxygen and NAD+ Precursors
*Speculative; no specific PD evidence.*

**Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO)** — breathing 100% oxygen at 1.5–2.5 atmospheres — increases dissolved plasma oxygen and may reduce neuroinflammation. Reported anecdotally and in small case series to reduce PD fatigue and improve cognition; no controlled trial data in PD.

**NAD+ precursors** (nicotinamide riboside, NR; nicotinamide mononucleotide, NMN) boost intracellular NAD+ levels and activate sirtuins and PARP pathways, supporting mitochondrial function and DNA repair. Highly relevant to PD biology in principle — mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced NAD+ metabolism are documented features of PD neurons. A small human study showed NR increased NAD+ in blood and CSF in PD patients. No clinical outcome trial exists.
