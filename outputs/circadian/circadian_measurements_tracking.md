# Measurements and Tracking
## Chapter 1 — Quantifying Circadian Health

---

This chapter defines how to assess circadian organization. The primer established that restoring circadian alignment is a high-leverage intervention for cancer risk and physiological coherence. Before optimizing, we must measure.

Circadian synchrony is evaluated by tracking physiological signals that capture **timing**, **strength**, **consistency**, and **coordination** of rhythms. These dimensions — phase, amplitude, stability, and cross-system alignment — allow quantitative evaluation of circadian health and guide iterative optimization.

---

## 1. Phase

Phase describes when circadian rhythms occur relative to biological night. It answers: *Is the system on time?*

### Primary reference: Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO)

The gold-standard phase marker. Measured via salivary melatonin sampled every 30–60 minutes in the evening under dim light (&lt;10 lux).

### Secondary phase markers

- Body temperature minimum  
- Sleep onset and sleep midpoint  
- Cortisol peak timing  
- Dawn glucose rise timing  

All phase relationships are expressed relative to DLMO.

---

## 2. Amplitude

Amplitude reflects the strength of day–night contrast. It answers: *How robust is the rhythm?*

Low amplitude indicates circadian weakening even when phase appears aligned. Assessed using:

- Melatonin nocturnal output  
- Temperature peak-to-trough difference  
- Rest–activity rhythm amplitude  
- Endocrine and metabolic day–night contrasts  

---

## 3. Stability

Stability reflects day-to-day reproducibility of circadian timing. It answers: *Is the system consistent?*

High variability indicates weak entrainment or internal desynchrony. Assessed using:

- Variance of DLMO timing across repeated measurements  
- Variance of temperature minimum timing  
- Variance of sleep midpoint across days  

---

## 4. Cross-System Alignment

Alignment reflects fixed phase relationships between circadian subsystems. It answers: *Are the clocks coordinated?*

Persistent deviations indicate internal circadian misalignment. Assessed using:

- Sleep timing relative to DLMO  
- Temperature minimum relative to sleep midpoint and DLMO  
- Cortisol dynamics relative to wake time and DLMO  
- Metabolic rhythms relative to biological night and day  

---

## 5. Tracked Readouts

Operational measurements used to derive phase, amplitude, stability, and alignment. Device types (not brand-specific):

- **Central timing** — Salivary melatonin for DLMO determination. *Device:* Saliva sampling kit + immunoassay (ELISA or RIA). Sample every 30–60 min in evening under dim light.
- **Thermoregulation** — Continuous body temperature. *Device:* Chest-worn patch or ingestible capsule for core; wrist heat-flux sensor as proxy. Core preferred; distal skin acceptable for relative rhythm.
- **Light exposure** — Continuous light sensing. *Device:* Wrist-worn or clip-on photosensor with spectral sensitivity; melanopic-weighted irradiance preferred over raw lux.
- **Behavior and sleep** — Rest–activity and sleep timing. *Device:* Wrist-worn actigraph (triaxial accelerometer). Supplement with sleep diary.
- **Endocrine rhythms** — Salivary cortisol. *Device:* Saliva sampling kit + immunoassay. Sample at waking (0, 15, 30, 45 min) and across the day.
- **Metabolic rhythms** — Glucose levels. *Device:* Continuous glucose monitor (CGM), subcutaneous sensor with real-time or scan-to-read output. Use standardized feeding conditions.

## 6. Circadian Health Benchmarks

Target values, acceptable ranges, and rationale. The [Circadian Tracking Table](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ZSRs-0XkmKa_bJxXlISOKpBUK7OAxhki/edit?gid=1665814829#gid=1665814829) operationalizes these for assessment and iteration.

### 6.1 Central Phase Stability

**DLMO stability** — Day-to-day variance of DLMO clock time across repeated measurements.

- **Optimal:** &lt;1 h SD across 3+ measurements.  
- **Rationale:** Low variance indicates strong entrainment and resistance to phase drift. High variance suggests weak zeitgeber input or conflicting cues.

### 6.2 Sleep–Circadian Alignment

**Sleep onset phase angle** — Interval from DLMO to sleep onset.

- **Optimal:** 2–3 h (sleep onset ~2–3 h after DLMO).  
- **Rationale:** In entrained individuals, DLMO typically precedes habitual sleep by 2–3 h. Deviations suggest misalignment between biological and behavioral night [[Rahman 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813580/); [Pullman 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22294806/)].

**Sleep midpoint phase angle** — Interval from DLMO to sleep midpoint.

- **Optimal:** ~4–5 h (sleep midpoint roughly mid-biological night).  
- **Rationale:** Sleep episode should center within biological night; large deviations indicate phase mismatch.

**Sleep midpoint stability** — Day-to-day SD of sleep midpoint.

- **Optimal:** &lt;1 h SD.  
- **Rationale:** Regular sleep timing supports entrainment; high variance reflects social jetlag or weak schedule anchor.

### 6.3 Temperature–Circadian Alignment

**Temperature minimum phase angle (relative to DLMO)** — Clock time of temp min minus DLMO.

- **Optimal:** Temp min ~7 h after DLMO.  
- **Rationale:** DLMO precedes temperature minimum by ~7 h in entrained adults; this phase relationship is used for light therapy and melatonin timing [[Lewy 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9920167/)].

**Temperature minimum phase angle (relative to sleep midpoint)** — Temp min should fall within sleep episode, typically in second half.

- **Optimal:** Temp min between sleep midpoint and wake.  
- **Rationale:** Core temperature declines through the night and reaches minimum near habitual wake; misalignment suggests desynchrony.

**Temperature rhythm amplitude** — Peak-to-trough difference (e.g., °C).

- **Optimal:** &gt;0.5°C; higher indicates stronger rhythm.  
- **Rationale:** Flattened amplitude reflects weak day–night contrast or peripheral desynchrony.

**Temperature minimum stability** — Day-to-day variance of temp min timing.

- **Optimal:** &lt;1 h SD.  
- **Rationale:** Stability reflects consistent peripheral clock coupling.

### 6.4 Rest–Activity Rhythmicity

**Relative amplitude** — Contrast between most active 10 h and least active 5 h (from actigraphy).

- **Optimal:** Higher = better; population norms vary by age. Low values (&lt;0.7) correlate with mortality [[Xu 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36450759/)].  
- **Rationale:** Amplitude reflects consolidation of activity into the biological day.

**Interdaily stability (IS)** — Consistency of 24 h pattern across days (0–1 scale).

- **Optimal:** Higher = better (IS &gt;0.6 suggests stable entrainment).  
- **Rationale:** Low IS indicates day-to-day irregularity; associated with poor health outcomes.

**Intradaily variability (IV)** — Fragmentation of activity within the day.

- **Optimal:** Lower = better (IV &lt;0.5).  
- **Rationale:** High IV indicates napping, irregular activity, or circadian fragmentation [[van Someren 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10436380/)].

### 6.5 Endocrine Rhythmicity

**Cortisol awakening response (CAR)** — Magnitude of cortisol rise 0–30 min post-wake.

- **Optimal:** 50–160% increase in salivary cortisol over 30 min; ~9 nmol/l rise typical in healthy adults. Blunted CAR (&lt;20% rise) suggests HPA dysregulation.  
- **Rationale:** CAR reflects SCN–HPA coupling; valid assessment requires strict timing from moment of wake [[Stalder 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26563991/)].

**Cortisol peak timing** — Time of maximal cortisol relative to wake.

- **Optimal:** Peak within 30–60 min of wake.  
- **Rationale:** Delayed peak suggests phase delay or HPA misalignment.

**Daytime cortisol decline** — Magnitude of decrease from peak to evening.

- **Optimal:** Clear decline across the day; flat profile indicates dysregulation.  
- **Rationale:** Diurnal rhythm separates biological day (high cortisol) from night (low).

**Evening cortisol suppression** — Evening levels low relative to morning.

- **Optimal:** Evening salivary cortisol &lt;50% of morning peak.  
- **Rationale:** Inadequate suppression blurs day–night separation.

### 6.6 Metabolic Rhythmicity

**Nocturnal glucose stability** — Coefficient of variation or SD of glucose during sleep window.

- **Optimal:** CV &lt;15–20%; low variability indicates metabolic quiescence.  
- **Rationale:** High nighttime variability suggests feeding interference or insulin dysregulation.

**Dawn glucose rise timing** — Clock time of glucose increase before wake.

- **Optimal:** Rise begins 1–2 h before habitual wake, aligning with circadian hepatic output.  
- **Rationale:** Dawn phenomenon reflects circadian regulation; mistimed rise indicates hepatic-clock desync.

**Day–night glucose contrast** — Difference between daytime and nocturnal mean or median.

- **Optimal:** Clear day–night difference (higher daytime, lower night).  
- **Rationale:** Flat profile suggests poor metabolic compartmentalization.

**Postprandial glucose dynamics** — Peak and time-to-baseline after standardized meal.

- **Optimal:** Context-dependent; circadian modulation means insulin sensitivity varies by time of day. Earlier meals often show better clearance.  
- **Rationale:** Mistimed eating relative to insulin sensitivity worsens excursions.

### 6.7 Cross-System Coherence

**Phase coherence** — Consistency of phase relationships across central, autonomic, behavioral, endocrine, and metabolic metrics.

- **Optimal:** All subsystems within expected phase relationships (e.g., temp min ~7 h after DLMO; cortisol peak near wake).  
- **Rationale:** Persistent deviations indicate internal desynchrony.

**Composite synchrony score** — Aggregated index of alignment, amplitude, stability, coherence.

- **Optimal:** Higher = better; thresholds are study-specific.  
- **Rationale:** Single-metric summary for global assessment and tracking over time.
