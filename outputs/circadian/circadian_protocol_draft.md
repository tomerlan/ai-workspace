# Circadian Entrainment Protocol

## Specification, Dosing, and Titration

---

## 1. Scope

This document specifies the intervention. The biological basis is in the foundations document; the assessment that precedes intervention and the criteria that define success are in the health document.

The protocol has one object: to restore a large, stable, correctly phased day–night signal, and to place behaviour in the correct relation to it. Every component below serves that object, and components are introduced in an order determined by how the oscillator responds rather than by convenience.

**Prerequisite.** Intervention is specified against a measured baseline. Applied without assessment, a light protocol has a substantial probability of being applied at the wrong phase, and light delivered at the wrong phase moves the oscillator in the wrong direction or degrades its amplitude. The minimum baseline is fourteen days of actigraphy with light recording and one central phase estimate.

---

## 2. Order of intervention

The dimensions of circadian health fail independently, but they do not respond independently, and the order of correction is fixed by three constraints.

**Stability precedes everything.** Phase and amplitude measured across an unstable baseline are not reliable quantities, and a variable schedule defeats any timed intervention because the intervention lands at a different internal phase each day.

**Amplitude precedes phase.** Phase lability is inversely related to amplitude, so a correction applied to a flattened oscillator will not hold. Raising the day–night contrast ratio also moves phase in the desired direction in a substantial fraction of cases without any timed intervention, which resolves the problem rather than deferring it.

**Phase precedes alignment.** The feeding window is positioned against central phase, so central phase must be known and stable before the window is set.

| Stage | Target | Primary action | Expected time to effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stability | Fix wake time; fix meal times | 3–7 days |
| 2 | Amplitude | Raise daytime dose; lower night exposure | 1–3 weeks |
| 3 | Phase | Timed light relative to temperature minimum; timed melatonin if required | 1 h/day maximum |
| 4 | Internal alignment | Compress and advance the feeding window | 1–2 weeks |

Stages overlap in practice. Stage 1 and stage 2 are begun together in most cases, because the actions do not conflict. Stage 3 is not begun until stages 1 and 2 have held for two weeks.

---

## 3. Time base

All timing in this protocol is specified against solar events and against the individual's measured temperature minimum, not against clock time.

**Why.** Clock time is an administrative convention. Solar noon within a single time zone varies by up to an hour across its width, and by more where zones are politically rather than geographically drawn; daylight saving displaces the whole population by a further hour twice a year; and the same clock hour corresponds to a different solar elevation in every season. A protocol written in clock hours is correct at one longitude on one date.

Kruse (2012, 2016) anchors all timing to sunrise, solar noon, and sunset on this ground, and treats position within the local photoperiod as the biologically operative variable.

**Working reference points.**

*Sunrise* anchors the morning exposure.

*Solar noon* anchors the ultraviolet-containing portion of the day and the peak of the feeding window.

*Sunset* anchors the beginning of the evening taper.

*Core temperature minimum (CBTmin)* divides the phase response curve and is the reference for all corrective light. In the absence of direct measurement it is estimated as DLMO plus 7 hours, or as habitual wake time minus 2 hours in a person with stable sleep.

---

## 4. Stage 1 — Stability

### 4.1 Fixed wake time

A single fixed wake time, seven days a week, including free days. Variance target under 30 minutes.

Wake time is the anchor rather than bedtime because it is the point at which the largest phase-setting stimulus is delivered, and because it is under voluntary control in a way that sleep onset is not. Bedtime is allowed to find its own position once wake time and light exposure are fixed.

Social jetlag is reduced by moving the free-day sleep midpoint toward the workday midpoint, not by moving the workday toward the weekend.

### 4.2 Fixed meal times

Meal times are fixed to the same tolerance as wake time before any change is made to the content or duration of the eating window. Irregular feeding is a source of peripheral phase instability independent of when the window sits.

### 4.3 Regularity of light exposure

The daily pattern of exposure is made regular before its magnitude is changed. A person who is outdoors for three hours on two days and none on five has a weaker effective zeitgeber than one outdoors for forty minutes daily.

---

## 5. Stage 2 — Amplitude

The operative quantity is the ratio between daytime and night-time melanopic exposure, and both halves are addressed. Correcting one alone is incomplete: raising daytime exposure while leaving evening light in place leaves the contrast compressed, and the reverse leaves the oscillator underdriven.

### 5.1 Morning anchor exposure

The highest-leverage single action in the protocol.

**Timing.** Beginning within 30–60 minutes of waking, and after sunrise. In a person with a stable schedule this falls safely after CBTmin, in the advance region of the phase response curve.

**Duration and dose.** Outdoors, 20–40 minutes. Overcast daylight delivers on the order of 10,000 lux and clear daylight far more, against 100–500 lux in a well-lit interior, so a short outdoor exposure exceeds an entire indoor day.

**If outdoor exposure is impossible.** A 10,000 lux therapeutic device at 30–60 cm for 20–30 minutes, positioned so that light enters the eye from above the visual axis, where ipRGC density is greatest. Devices are specified by melanopic EDI at the eye rather than by nominal lux at the source.

**Windows do not substitute.** Ordinary glazing removes ultraviolet almost entirely and attenuates the short-wavelength visible band, and indoor illuminance beside a window is typically one to two orders of magnitude below outdoor. Kruse (2016, 2019) treats glass, spectacles, contact lenses, and sunglasses as filters that remove components of the signal, and specifies unfiltered exposure where conditions permit it.

**Spectral composition.** Melanopic weighting does not fully specify the stimulus, because melanopsin photo-regenerates and long wavelengths return the pigment toward its resting state. A broadband source and a blue-enriched, red-depleted source of equal melanopic EDI are not equivalent. Daylight is broadband; most therapeutic devices are not.

### 5.2 Daytime dose

**Target.** At least 250 lux melanopic EDI at eye level throughout the waking day, and a cumulative total of at least two hours above 1,000 lux.

**Method.** Exposure is accumulated in several short outdoor intervals rather than pursued as a single block. The dominant determinant of outdoor melanopic dose is the solid angle of visible sky, not direct sun, so an open aspect matters more than sunshine and overcast conditions remain effective.

**Diminishing returns.** The irradiance–response relation saturates. Beyond the threshold range, additional daytime illuminance adds little, and effort is better directed at the evening half of the ratio.

### 5.3 Evening taper

**Timing.** Begins 3 hours before intended sleep onset, and after sunset.

**Target.** Below 10 lux melanopic EDI at the eye.

**Method.** The taper is achieved primarily by geometry and secondarily by spectrum. Overhead lighting is extinguished and replaced by low-mounted sources below eye level, which reduces the dose reaching the inferior retina where ipRGC input is weighted. Intensity is reduced first, colour temperature second. Where screens cannot be avoided, brightness is reduced to the minimum usable level and the display is positioned below the visual axis.

**Blue-blocking eyewear.** Effective where the environment cannot be controlled. Amber lenses attenuating below approximately 530 nm are required; lightly tinted lenses marketed for screen use do not reach the necessary attenuation.

**Steepness at the low end.** The irradiance–response curve is steepest at low light levels, so the reduction from ordinary evening lighting to dim carries more effect than any further reduction, and the first hour of taper carries more than the third.

### 5.4 Sleep-period darkness

**Target.** Below 1 lux melanopic EDI.

Blackout of external sources, removal of standby indicators, and no illumination during night waking. Where light is required for safety, a low-mounted long-wavelength source at minimum intensity.

### 5.5 Spectral sequence across the day

Kruse (2016, 2022) treats the ordered progression of the solar spectrum — red and infrared at low solar elevation, ultraviolet A as elevation rises, ultraviolet B only near solar noon — as itself the signal, on the ground that no single component reproduces the sequence and that the timing of each component relative to the others carries information that total illuminance does not.

The corresponding practice is exposure distributed across the day rather than concentrated: near sunrise, near solar noon, and near sunset. The sunrise and sunset exposures are low in intensity and rich in long wavelengths and occur at phases where the system is most sensitive to the transition; the midday exposure is the only one containing ultraviolet B.

## 6. Stage 3 — Phase correction

Undertaken only when stability and amplitude have held for two weeks, and only against a measured phase.

### 6.1 The rule

Light after CBTmin advances phase. Light before CBTmin delays it. The transition is abrupt and sits close to the temperature minimum, which is why an unmeasured CBTmin makes the intervention a coin toss: an exposure intended to advance a delayed sleeper, delivered an hour too early, delays them further.

### 6.2 To advance a delayed phase

Bright light on waking, at a wake time held constant or moved earlier by no more than 30 minutes per day. Strict evening taper from 3 hours before sleep. Where required, melatonin 0.5 mg taken 5–6 hours before habitual sleep onset — a phase-shifting dose acting through the melatonin phase response curve, which is roughly the inverse of the light curve, and unrelated to the larger doses used for hypnotic effect.

Achievable rate: approximately 1 hour per day, often less in a markedly delayed individual.

### 6.3 To delay an advanced phase

Bright light in the evening, before CBTmin. Strict avoidance of morning light until the target phase is approached. Morning melatonin is not used.

### 6.4 The intensity constraint near the temperature minimum

A stimulus strong enough delivered near enough to the critical phase drives the oscillator toward zero amplitude rather than to a new phase; bright light close to CBTmin can suppress the amplitude of the subsequent melatonin rhythm rather than shift it.

Phase-shifting exposures are therefore specified by intensity as well as timing, and high-intensity exposure within approximately an hour of the estimated temperature minimum is avoided in favour of exposure displaced clearly to one side of it.

### 6.5 Intermittent exposure

Intensity and duration are not fully interchangeable. Sequences of brief bright pulses produce phase shifts approaching those of continuous exposure of the same total duration, because the pathway responds more strongly to onset than to maintenance. Four 15-minute exposures distributed across an hour are a practical substitute for a continuous hour.

## 7. Stage 4 — Internal alignment

### 7.1 The feeding window

**Position.** Contained within the light period. First intake within 1–2 hours of waking; last intake at least 3 hours before sleep onset and, where DLMO is known, before DLMO.

**Duration.** 10–12 hours initially, compressed toward 8–10 hours if tolerated and if the position is already correct. Position is corrected before duration is reduced: an 8-hour window placed late is worse than a 12-hour window placed early.

**Distribution.** Energy weighted toward the earlier part of the window. Glucose tolerance is highest in the morning and declines across the day, so identical intake carries a larger glycaemic cost later.

Kruse (2012, 2024) specifies intake concentrated after sunrise with the largest meal early and intake tapering across the day, terminating some hours before sleep, subordinating meal timing to the light cycle rather than treating the two as independent.

### 7.2 Why the window is set last

The window is positioned against central phase. Set before phase is known and stable, it is positioned against a moving reference and requires resetting.

### 7.3 Conditional interventions

Kruse (2019) holds that fasting, time-restricted feeding, and cold exposure act through circadian mechanisms and are therefore conditional on those mechanisms functioning — that they are gated by the light environment rather than independent of it.

The corresponding practice is that extended fasting, aggressive window compression, and cold exposure are not introduced as first-line measures in a person whose light environment has not been corrected. They are added, if at all, after stages 1 through 3 have held.

### 7.4 Secondary zeitgebers

**Physical activity.** Exercise has its own phase response curve, with morning and late-afternoon exercise advancing phase and late-evening exercise delaying it. It is used to reinforce the direction set by light, not against it.

**Temperature.** A falling core temperature promotes sleep onset. A warm bath 1–2 hours before bed accelerates subsequent heat loss and advances sleep onset. Bedroom temperature is kept low.

**Meal composition** is secondary to meal timing for circadian purposes.

## 8. Implementation under constraint

### 8.1 The indoor worker

The default case, and the one in which amplitude reduction dominates.

Morning exposure outdoors before commuting; commuting itself used as exposure where possible; a deliberate outdoor interval near solar noon; workstation positioned for maximum daylight; evening taper beginning at a fixed hour. The single highest-yield change is usually the midday outdoor interval, because it is the largest available increment and is habitually omitted.

### 8.2 High latitude and winter

Where the photoperiod is short and solar elevation is low, the natural signal may not reach threshold. A 10,000 lux device is used at the fixed wake time to substitute for the missing morning signal, and outdoor exposure at midday is retained because daylight remains far brighter than any interior even under heavy overcast. The evening taper is extended, since the interval between sunset and sleep is long.

The seasonal reduction in ultraviolet B at high latitude is absolute: at latitudes above approximately 37°, ultraviolet B sufficient for cutaneous vitamin D synthesis is unavailable for part of the year regardless of exposure duration.

### 8.3 Shift work

Full inversion is attempted only for permanent night schedules with consistent sleep timing on days off. For rotating shifts, full re-entrainment is not achievable between rotations, and the objective is limited to protecting sleep and amplitude.

**Permanent night schedule.** Bright light during the first half of the shift; dark glasses on the commute home; blackout bedroom; fixed sleep period; light avoidance before sleep and bright light on waking into the evening.

**Rotating schedule.** An anchor sleep period held constant across all rotations, bright light during the shift, and darkness protection for sleep, without attempting to move phase.

### 8.4 Transmeridian travel

Natural re-entrainment proceeds at roughly 1 hour per day eastward and 1.5 hours per day westward. Pre-flight shifting of 1 hour per day for two to three days reduces the residual.

On arrival, exposure is timed against the estimated position of CBTmin in the traveller's still-shifted internal time rather than against local morning. For eastward travel in the first days, local morning light frequently falls before internal CBTmin and delays rather than advances; morning light is avoided until internal CBTmin has passed.

### 8.5 Delayed and advanced sleep phase

The protocols of section 6, applied over weeks rather than days, with the recognition that the intrinsic period and light sensitivity of these individuals differ from the population and that relapse on discontinuation is expected. Maintenance exposure is continued indefinitely.

### 8.6 Total blindness without light perception

The photic pathway is unavailable and the protocol reduces to non-photic measures: rigidly fixed schedule, fixed meal times, and melatonin or a melatonin receptor agonist timed to the individual's drifting phase.

### 8.7 Older adults

Amplitude declines and phase advances with age, and lens yellowing reduces short-wavelength transmission so that a given illuminance delivers a smaller effective stimulus. Daytime dose targets are raised. Where cataract surgery has occurred, transmission is restored and the requirement falls.

## 9. Safety and contraindications

**Bipolar disorder.** Bright light therapy can precipitate mania or mixed states. Use requires psychiatric supervision, and morning bright light is introduced at reduced duration with monitoring.

**Photosensitising medication and ocular disease.** Amiodarone, tetracyclines, phenothiazines, psoralens, some antibiotics, and St John's wort raise photosensitivity. Existing retinal disease, and recent retinal surgery, require ophthalmological advice before high-intensity exposure.

**Ultraviolet exposure.** Solar ultraviolet is a Group 1 human carcinogen, and the protocol's midday exposure component carries genuine risk that scales with dose, skin phototype, and cumulative history. The evidence separates two patterns: intermittent intense exposure producing sunburn is the pattern associated with melanoma, and cumulative dose is the pattern associated with keratinocyte cancers. Sub-erythemal exposure is the operative constraint — exposure is kept below the dose that produces any erythema, which for lighter phototypes at high solar elevation is a small number of minutes. Personal and family history of skin cancer, immunosuppression, and photosensitising conditions are contraindications to the ultraviolet component specifically, and the remainder of the protocol is unaffected by its removal.

Kruse's position favours substantially greater ultraviolet exposure than dermatological guidance supports. The tension is real and is not resolved by the evidence available. The sub-erythemal constraint is retained here as the boundary condition.

**Melatonin.** The phase-shifting dose is 0.5 mg and its effect depends on timing; larger doses do not shift phase more effectively and produce daytime carryover. Interactions include warfarin and fluvoxamine. Not used in pregnancy without advice.

**Phase correction without measurement.** Timed light applied against an unmeasured phase can move the oscillator in the direction opposite to that intended. Where phase cannot be measured, stages 1 and 2 are applied and stage 3 is omitted.

## 10. Titration and reassessment

**Reassessment interval.** Fourteen days of actigraphy with light recording after each stage, and a repeat central phase estimate before and after stage 3.

**Advancement criterion.** A stage is held until its target criterion in the health document is met and has held for two weeks. Stages are not run in parallel beyond the overlap of 1 and 2.

**Expected time course.** Regularity responds within days. Amplitude responds over one to three weeks. Phase moves at up to 1 hour per day and usually less. Metabolic markers follow the rhythm rather than leading it, over weeks to months.

**Seasonal comparison.** Reassessment is compared against a baseline matched for season and latitude, since photoperiod alone moves phase, amplitude, and the achievable daytime dose.

---

## 11. Failure analysis

When the criteria are not met after a stage has been applied as specified, the causes fall into a small number of classes.

**Measured exposure does not match intended exposure.** The most common finding. The light record is checked before any change to the prescription, since wrist-worn sensors underestimate exposure at the eye and self-report overestimates it.

**Phase moving in the wrong direction.** The exposure is falling on the wrong side of CBTmin. The temperature minimum estimate is re-derived, preferably from a repeat DLMO rather than from wake time.

**Amplitude not improving despite adequate daytime dose.** The night half of the ratio is the constraint. Sleep-period and evening exposure are measured rather than assumed.

**Phase correct, alignment not improving.** The feeding window is the constraint, or an unrecorded intake is present. The intake log is checked against the CGM record for excursions without a corresponding entry.

**Everything correct, symptoms unchanged.** The presenting problem is not primarily circadian. The protocol has a defined endpoint, and reaching it without symptomatic benefit is an indication to look elsewhere rather than to intensify.

## 12. Summary specification

| Component | Specification | Reference point |
|---|---|---|
| Wake time | Fixed, 7 days/week, variance <30 min | — |
| Morning exposure | 20–40 min outdoors, unfiltered | Within 30–60 min of waking, after sunrise |
| Daytime dose | ≥250 lx mEDI sustained; ≥2 h above 1,000 lx | Distributed, including near solar noon |
| Ultraviolet component | Sub-erythemal only, phototype-adjusted | Near solar noon |
| Evening taper | <10 lx mEDI; overhead lighting off | From 3 h before sleep, after sunset |
| Sleep-period darkness | <1 lx mEDI | Sleep period |
| Feeding window | 10–12 h, energy weighted early | First intake within 1–2 h of waking; last ≥3 h before sleep, before DLMO |
| Phase correction | Light after CBTmin to advance, before to delay; ≤1 h/day | CBTmin |
| Melatonin, if used | 0.5 mg | 5–6 h before habitual sleep onset |
| Exercise | Morning or late afternoon | Reinforcing the direction set by light |
