# Circadian Organization and Cancer: Theory and Practical Guidelines

## 0. Field Definition and Scope

**Chronobiology** — Chronobiology is the study of endogenous biological timing systems and how they interact with external environmental cycles such as light–dark patterns, feeding, temperature, and activity. These timing systems regulate when biological processes occur, not just how they occur.

**Circadian biology** — Circadian biology is the branch of chronobiology focused on approximately 24-hour oscillatory systems. These systems coordinate physiology across multiple scales, from gene expression and metabolism within individual cells to behavior, hormone secretion, immune function, and tissue repair at the organismal level.

Circadian organization ensures that incompatible processes—such as DNA replication and repair, anabolism and catabolism, inflammation and regeneration—are temporally separated rather than occurring simultaneously.

**Central thesis** — Circadian organization functions as a global constraint system on cellular behavior. By imposing temporal order, it limits the degrees of freedom available to cells and tissues.

Cancer can be understood, in part, as a failure of this temporal constraint. When circadian signals weaken, fragment, or become misaligned, cells gain inappropriate temporal freedom: proliferation, metabolism, stress responses, and immune evasion overlap in ways that increase the likelihood of malignant growth. From this perspective, cancer progression is not driven solely by genetic mutations, but also by the erosion of system-level timing control.

## 1. Internal Timekeepers (Oscillators)

Biological systems contain self-sustaining clocks that generate time internally, independent of external cues. These oscillators set the temporal structure of physiology, while requiring separate input pathways to align their timing to the environment.

### 1.1 Molecular clocks (cell-autonomous)

- Present in almost all nucleated cells
- Core TTFL (CLOCK/BMAL1 ↔ PER/CRY)
- Accessory loops (REV-ERB/ROR)
- Control rhythmic expression of:
  - cell cycle regulators
  - DNA repair genes
  - mitochondrial enzymes
  - antioxidant systems
  - immune mediators

**Key property:** They oscillate even in isolation, but phase coherence is fragile.

### 1.2 SCN (Suprachiasmatic Nucleus)

- Network oscillator (~20k neurons)
- Electrical rhythm + molecular clocks
- Receives direct photic input
- Most phase-stable oscillator in the organism

**Function:** Acts as phase authority, not execution engine.

### 1.3 Peripheral clocks

- Liver, gut, muscle, adipose, immune cells, tumors
- Highly sensitive to local signals
- Can desynchronize from SCN

**Cancer-relevant fact:** Tumors frequently retain clock genes but lose clock coupling.

## 2. External Zeitgebers (Primary Inputs)

Environmental signals such as light, feeding, and activity do not generate time themselves, but reset and synchronize internal clocks to the external world by shifting circadian phase.

### 2.1 Light (dominant)

- Retina → melanopsin → SCN
- Phase-dependent effects (PRC)
- Overrides sleep, hormones, behavior
- Light at night suppresses melatonin and delays phase

**Hierarchy position:** Top of the system.

### 2.2 Food timing

- Strong entrainer of liver, gut, pancreas
- Weak effect on SCN
- Major source of internal desynchrony

**Cancer relevance:** Night feeding creates metabolic states permissive to proliferation.

### 2.3 Physical activity

- Moderate entrainer
- Works via temperature and metabolism
- Phase-dependent

### 2.4 Social / behavioral cues

- Weak entrainers
- Supportive but not authoritative

---

## 3. Internal Non-Photic Entrainers (Local)

Signals such as feeding, temperature, and activity timing influence the strength and coordination of local clocks, shaping amplitude and internal coherence without overriding the central circadian phase.

- Redox cofactors (NAD⁺/NADH)
- ROS oscillations
- Cellular energy charge (ATP/AMP)
- Temperature cycles
- Microbial metabolites (SCFAs, bile acids)

**Important distinction:** These signals entrain locally and can oppose SCN timing.

---

## 4. Long-Range Circadian Signals

Once time is generated and set, it must be communicated across the organism. Long-range circadian signals transmit timing information between tissues, coordinating physiology so that distant systems act in the correct temporal relationship.

### 4.1 Melatonin

- Pineal-derived
- Signals biological night
- Acts via:
  - endocrine distribution
  - central feedback to SCN
  - mitochondrial protection
- Coordinates peripheral clocks

**Cancer relevance:**
- Reinforces repair-dominant states
- Constrains proliferation temporally
- Often ignored or resisted by tumors

### 4.2 Cortisol

- SCN → HPA axis
- Signals biological morning
- Mobilizes energy
- Anti-inflammatory timing gate

**Cancer relevance:**
- Flattened rhythms predict worse outcomes
- Timing matters more than absolute level

### 4.3 Autonomic rhythms

- Sympathetic/parasympathetic cycling
- Fast, organ-specific timing
- Controls heart rate, gut motility, liver glucose output

### 4.4 Body temperature rhythm

- Ancient, ubiquitous signal
- Weak but universal entrainer
- Couples cellular biochemistry to time

---

## 5. Hierarchy and Override Rules

Circadian control is organized hierarchically: some signals dominate others, and certain inputs can override local timing under specific conditions. Understanding these rules clarifies which cues set master phase, which modulate downstream systems, and when conflicts lead to desynchrony rather than adaptation.

**Absolute hierarchy (top → bottom)**

- Light → SCN
- SCN → neural + endocrine outputs
- Melatonin / temperature → peripheral clocks
- Feeding → peripheral clocks
- Local metabolic signals

**Override principles**

- Light overrides melatonin
- Feeding overrides liver clock
- No single signal overrides everything downstream
- Chronic conflict = desynchrony

---

## 6. Circadian Health vs Desynchrony

Circadian health reflects stable alignment between internal clocks and external time, with strong rhythms and coherent phase relationships across systems. Desynchrony arises when timing cues conflict or weaken, leading to drift, reduced amplitude, and loss of coordination between biological processes.

### 6.1 Circadian health

- Stable phase
- High amplitude
- Low noise
- Tight internal coherence

### 6.2 Circadian desynchrony

Loss of agreement between:

- SCN and periphery
- Organs with each other
- Signals and behavior

**Key insight:** Clocks still run — they disagree.

### 6.3 Common disruptors

- Light at night
- Shift work
- Irregular sleep
- Night eating
- Critical illness
- Chronic stress
- Aging

## 7. Mechanistic Links to Cancer

Circadian systems constrain when cells divide, repair damage, and manage energy. When temporal control weakens or becomes misaligned, these constraints erode, creating conditions that favor genomic instability, dysregulated metabolism, chronic inflammation, and malignant progression.

### 7.1 Temporal overlap of incompatible processes

- Proliferation + inflammation
- DNA replication + oxidative stress
- Feeding + insulin resistance
- → Increased error rates.

### 7.2 Loss of repair windows

- DNA repair mistimed
- Autophagy reduced
- Mitophagy impaired

### 7.3 Immune mis-timing

- Reduced nighttime immune coordination
- Chronic inflammatory tone

### 7.4 Metabolic permissiveness

- Glycolytic bias
- Mitochondrial fragmentation
- Loss of metabolic gating

### 7.5 Tissue architecture instability

- Rhythmic adhesion molecule expression lost
- Increased invasion and metastasis

## 8. Evidence

This section summarizes the empirical support for the framework, prioritizing reproducible findings from human studies, animal models, and mechanistic experiments, while clearly separating established results from extrapolation.

### 8.1. Epidemiological

Chronic circadian disruption from long-term night shift work, irregular sleep–wake timing, or nocturnal light exposure is consistently associated with increased cancer incidence and worse prognosis.

- Large-scale population studies support the classification of circadian disruption as a probable carcinogen.
- Blunted or irregular rest–activity rhythms in patients correlate with reduced survival and accelerated disease progression.

### 8.2. Controlled Human Disruption Studies

Experimental misalignment of behavioral and circadian time in humans induces cancer-relevant changes, including suppression of rhythmic gene expression in DNA repair pathways and accumulation of DNA damage.

Short-term simulated shift work disrupts clock-controlled transcriptional programs related to cell cycle, immune regulation, and metabolism.

### 8.3. Human Chronotherapy and Rhythm Interventions

**Treatment Timing:** Administering cancer therapies at circadian-preferred phases improves tolerability and can enhance efficacy. Clinical trials using chronomodulated chemotherapy show reduced toxicity and longer survival compared to standard schedules.

**Structured Behavioral Rhythms:** Interventions that stabilize circadian phase (fixed wake times, timed light exposure, regular activity) improve sleep quality and quality of life. Observational evidence links rhythm consolidation with longer survival.

**Phase-Referenced Scheduling:** Therapeutic timing must be personalized to internal circadian markers, as the optimal time varies by chronotype, rhythm stability, and tumor biology.

### 8.4. Environmental and Genetic Animal Models

Environmental disruption (e.g., chronic jet lag, light at night, rotating light–dark schedules) accelerates tumor growth, impairs immune responses, and increases metastatic potential in rodents.

Genetic disruption of core clock genes modifies tumor initiation and progression; outcomes vary by gene and context but commonly reflect loss of temporal constraint over cell division, DNA repair, apoptosis, and immune surveillance.

Disruption-induced tumors exhibit increased "stemness," immunosuppression, and metabolic deregulation.

### 8.5. Reversibility and Circadian Restoration in Preclinical Models

**Light structure and melatonin:** Restoring light–dark cycles and preserving nocturnal melatonin rhythms suppress tumor growth and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity. Melatonin's efficacy depends on preserved circadian amplitude.

**Feeding–fasting structure:** Time-restricted feeding aligned with the active phase inhibits tumor growth, improves immune infiltration, reduces systemic toxicity, and enhances treatment response.

**Exercise timing:** Exercise during the biological active phase improves outcomes when combined with therapy, supporting peripheral clock reinforcement.

**Hormonal chronobiotics:** Exogenous melatonin reduces tumor growth and chemotherapy toxicity in rhythm-stabilized systems.

**Pharmacologic clock modulation:** Small molecules that stabilize or shift core clock components reduce tumor proliferation and reprogram metabolic and immune pathways.

Restoration of rhythms reduces dissemination, reestablishes checkpoint timing, and shifts gene expression in favor of tumor suppression.

### 8.6. Time-of-Day Dependence of Therapy

Drug pharmacokinetics (absorption, metabolism) and pharmacodynamics (target sensitivity, toxicity thresholds) are circadian-regulated.

Cytotoxicity of therapies varies by dosing time; these effects are lost in clock-deficient conditions, indicating clock mediation.

Both host and tumor clock state influence therapeutic window and efficacy.

Chronotherapy benefits are inconsistent across studies due to interindividual rhythm variation, drug class, sex, and tumor biology.

### 8.7. Mechanistic Support from Experimental Systems

Circadian clocks regulate multiple cancer-relevant pathways: cell cycle checkpoints, DNA repair fidelity, apoptotic thresholds, mitochondrial dynamics, metabolic flux, immune gating, adhesion, and angiogenesis.

Clock gene manipulation alters tumor behavior by shifting transcriptional control over oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways (e.g., c-MYC, p53, WEE1, mTOR).

Temporal misalignment permits overlap of incompatible processes (e.g., DNA replication during oxidative peaks), increasing error load and tumor permissiveness.

Loss of circadian gating reduces precision in stress responses and weakens tumor containment systems at the molecular and systemic level.

## 9. Kruse

This section outlines Jack Kruse's circadian framework, which emphasizes light environment, spectral quality, and environmental context as dominant regulators of circadian amplitude and metabolic state, extending beyond conventional chronobiology into hypothesis-driven territory.

### 9.1. Light Timing and Spectral Control

Morning natural light, particularly red-rich sunrise spectra, is framed as a primary circadian activator and metabolic start signal.

Light at night, especially blue-enriched artificial light, is treated as a central error signal that desynchronizes physiological timing.

Spectral mismatch between indoor environments and natural light cycles is proposed to disrupt mitochondrial day/night mode switching.

True darkness during sleep is treated as essential for repair-phase activation and redox resetting.

### 9.2. Leptin–Melanocortin Axis as Circadian–Metabolic Integrator

Leptin is positioned as a central environmental energy-status signal linking light exposure and metabolism.

The hypothalamic melanocortin system (POMC–α-MSH–MC4R) is framed as a global timing integrator that synchronizes mitochondrial energy partitioning.

Leptin resistance is hypothesized to originate more from circadian misalignment and spectral disruption than caloric excess.

Melanocortin signaling is proposed to determine peripheral mitochondrial coupling status (e.g., heat vs ATP production).

### 9.3. Mitochondria as Executors of Circadian Programs

Mitochondria are described as the downstream effectors of circadian control, executing day vs night metabolic states.

The daytime phase is linked to ATP production and controlled ROS signaling; the nighttime phase is characterized by repair-biased, endergonic processes.

Electron transport chain uncoupling is framed as a tunable variable affecting thermal output, redox status, and repair gating.

### 9.4. Melatonin and the Nocturnal Redox Program

Melatonin is positioned not only as a circadian hormone but as a mitochondrial antioxidant that gates nighttime repair.

It is proposed to interact directly with Complex I to reduce ROS generation during high-electron-pressure states.

Nighttime physiology is associated with mitophagy, fusion-biased dynamics, and redox normalization.

Sleep pressure is linked to mitochondrial oxidative signatures, integrating redox history with sleep induction.

### 9.5. Cancer as a Failure of Temporal and Redox Control

Clock gene disruption is framed as permissive for oncogenesis via loss of timing over proliferation, DNA repair, and immune activation.

Flattened circadian amplitude is treated as a tumor-enabling environment.

The causal hierarchy is inverted: mitochondria are viewed as executing clock outputs, not merely downstream responders.

Cancer metabolism (e.g., Warburg effect) is interpreted as a regression to a default glycolytic state arising from circadian and redox collapse.

Restoration of temporal order is proposed as necessary infrastructure for sustained tumor suppression.

### 9.6. Solar Conditioning and Sequential Phase Exposure

System resilience is tied to proper sequencing of solar input: red/infrared priming (morning), UV-induced adaptive stress (midday), and darkness/cooling (night).

Missing or scrambling these phases is hypothesized to suppress repair programs and exaggerate damage from otherwise tolerable exposures.

Morning light is viewed as preconditioning against UV-induced genomic stress later in the day.

## 10. Zaid

This section presents Zaid K. Dahhaj's circadian perspective, which emphasizes strict temporal discipline, minimization of circadian "noise," and the view that health depends on maintaining clear separation and coherence between biological day and biological night.

- Artificial light at night substantially counteracts the benefits of daytime sunlight
- Circadian responses are non-linear, with stabilization thresholds
- Sunburn reflects exceeded adaptive capacity rather than UV dose alone
- Sunlight functions hormetically when progressively applied

### 10.1. Morning phase (priming and protection)

- Morning light acts as a circadian warm-up
- Morning UVA initiates melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH) signaling
- Morning light enhances DNA repair capacity before peak UV
- Morning red/infrared light preconditions mitochondria
- Skipping morning sun increases vulnerability to damage

### 10.2. Midday phase (adaptive stress)

- Controlled UV stress builds genomic resilience
- Midday UV educates immune tolerance
- Midday sun induces autophagy and apoptosis

### 10.3. Night phase (repair and containment)

- Melatonin enhances mitophagy and reverses UV-induced cellular damage
- Circadian disruption reduces temporal precision of p53 activation
- Loss of melatonin silences p53 under real-world conditions
- Circadian disruption creates chronic oxidative stress
- Darkness and nighttime cooling favor repair-dominant metabolic states
- Circadian disruption alone is sufficient for carcinogenesis
- Skipping any circadian phase reduces overall adaptive benefit

### 10.4. Mechanistic implications for cancer

- Melatonin-mediated mitochondrial protection is central to cancer prevention
- Light at night abolishes cortisol rhythms, contributing to tumor progression
- Circadian disruption promotes invasion and metastasis
- Clock disruption biases mitochondria toward fragmented, cancer-like states
- Cell adhesion molecules (integrins, cadherins, selectins) are rhythmically expressed
- Circadian misalignment loosens adhesion and facilitates metastasis
- Hypoxia responses become locked in a pro-tumor state
- Exosome release and cargo composition show circadian rhythms
- Circadian disruption shifts exosome signaling toward pro-tumor patterns

### 10.5. Overarching principle

- Sunlight is the dominant synchronizer of anti-cancer defenses
- UV avoidance meaningfully increases cancer risk relative to controlled exposure

## 11. Measurement and tracking

Circadian synchrony is assessed by tracking key physiological signals that capture timing, strength, consistency, and coordination of circadian rhythms. Together, these measurements allow quantitative evaluation of phase, amplitude, stability, and alignment across systems.

### 11.1. Phase

Timing of circadian rhythms relative to biological night. **Primary reference:**

Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO), measured from salivary melatonin sampled every 30–60 minutes in the evening under <10 lux.

**Secondary phase markers:**

- Body temperature minimum
- Sleep onset and sleep midpoint
- Cortisol peak timing
- Dawn glucose rise timing

All phase relationships are expressed relative to DLMO.

### 11.2. Amplitude

Reflects strength of day–night contrast. Assessed using:

- Melatonin nocturnal output
- Temperature peak-to-trough difference
- Rest–activity rhythm amplitude
- Endocrine and metabolic day–night contrasts

Low values indicate circadian weakening even when timing appears aligned.

### 11.3. Stability

Reflects day-to-day reproducibility of circadian timing. Assessed using:

- Variance of DLMO timing across repeated measurements
- Variance of temperature minimum timing
- Variance of sleep midpoint across days

High variability indicates weak entrainment or internal desynchrony.

### 11.4. Cross-System Alignment

Reflects fixed phase relationships between circadian subsystems. Assessed using:

- Sleep timing relative to DLMO
- Temperature minimum relative to sleep midpoint and DLMO
- Cortisol dynamics relative to wake time and DLMO
- Metabolic rhythms relative to biological night and day

Persistent deviations indicate internal circadian misalignment.

### 11.5. Tracked Readouts

- **Central timing:** Salivary melatonin for DLMO determination
- **Thermoregulation:** Continuous body temperature monitoring (core preferred; distal skin as proxy)
- **Light exposure:** Continuous wearable light sensing with illuminance and melanopic-weighted estimates
- **Behavior and sleep:** Actigraphy for rest–activity rhythms and sleep timing, supplemented by sleep diary
- **Endocrine rhythms:** Salivary cortisol sampled at waking and across the day
- **Metabolic rhythms:** Continuous glucose monitoring under standardized feeding conditions

All quantitative measurements, derived metrics, target ranges, and deviation calculations are defined and evaluated using the [Circadian Tracking Table](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ZSRs-0XkmKa_bJxXlISOKpBUK7OAxhki/edit?gid=1665814829#gid=1665814829). The table operationalizes the measurement framework described here and serves as the reference for assessment, visualization, and iteration.

## 12. Circadian Health Benchmarks

Circadian health is evaluated using metrics derived from core circadian measurements. Each metric captures a specific aspect of temporal organization. Quantitative target values and acceptable ranges are defined externally in the analysis table.

### 12.1. Central Phase Stability

**DLMO stability:** Day-to-day reproducibility of dim-light melatonin onset. Reflects strength of central clock entrainment and resistance to phase drift.

### 12.2. Sleep–Circadian Alignment

**Sleep onset phase angle:** Interval between dim-light melatonin onset and sleep onset. Quantifies alignment between biological night and behavioral sleep initiation.

**Sleep midpoint phase angle:** Interval between dim-light melatonin onset and sleep midpoint. Reflects integration of sleep timing with central circadian phase.

**Sleep midpoint stability:** Day-to-day variability of sleep midpoint. Captures behavioral regularity under a fixed protocol.

### 12.3. Temperature–Circadian Alignment

**Temperature minimum phase angle relative to DLMO:** Timing of the body temperature minimum relative to biological night. Reflects autonomic and thermoregulatory coupling to the central clock.

**Temperature minimum phase angle relative to sleep midpoint:** Timing of the temperature minimum within the sleep episode. Indicates internal coordination between sleep propensity and thermoregulation.

**Temperature rhythm amplitude:** Peak-to-trough difference of the body temperature rhythm. Reflects robustness of autonomic circadian output.

**Temperature minimum stability:** Day-to-day variability of temperature minimum timing. Captures stability of peripheral clock expression.

### 12.4. Rest–Activity Rhythmicity

**Relative amplitude:** Contrast between active and rest phases in the actigraphy signal. Reflects consolidation of behavioral rhythms.

**Interdaily stability:** Consistency of the activity pattern across days. Reflects behavioral entrainment.

**Intradaily variability:** Fragmentation of the activity rhythm within a day. High values indicate circadian disruption.

### 12.5. Endocrine Rhythmicity

**Cortisol awakening response:** Magnitude of cortisol rise following awakening. Reflects integrity of circadian–hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis coupling.

**Cortisol peak timing:** Timing of maximal cortisol relative to wake time. Reflects phase alignment of endocrine output.

**Daytime cortisol decline:** Magnitude of cortisol reduction across the day. Reflects diurnal regulation and circadian modulation.

**Evening cortisol suppression:** Degree of cortisol suppression in the evening relative to morning peak. Reflects separation of biological day and night.

### 12.6. Metabolic Rhythmicity

**Nocturnal glucose stability:** Variability of glucose levels during biological night. Reflects metabolic quiescence.

**Dawn glucose rise timing:** Timing of glucose increase preceding wake. Reflects circadian regulation of hepatic glucose output.

**Day–night glucose contrast:** Difference between daytime and nocturnal glucose levels. Reflects circadian metabolic compartmentalization.

**Postprandial glucose dynamics:** Magnitude and recovery time of glucose excursions under standardized meals. Reflects circadian modulation of insulin sensitivity.

### 12.7. Cross-System Coherence

**Phase coherence:** Consistency of phase relationships across central, autonomic, behavioral, endocrine, and metabolic metrics. Reflects internal circadian synchrony.

**Composite synchrony score:** Aggregated measure integrating phase alignment, amplitude, stability, and coherence. Used for global assessment and visualization.

## 13. Circadian Optimization Protocol

The protocol defines controllable zeitgebers and masking variables used to stabilize, probe, and optimize circadian synchrony. Adjustments are made iteratively based on measured circadian metrics.

### 13.1. Tier 1 — Core Protocol (Evidence-Based)

**Wake-time anchor** — **Protocol:** Fixed wake time every day; bedtime flexible but sufficient to allow adequate sleep duration. **Rationale:** Wake time most strongly anchors central circadian phase and reduces phase noise.

**Morning outdoor light** — **Protocol:** Outdoor light exposure shortly after waking; eyes exposed to ambient daylight; artificial fallback if unavailable. **Rationale:** Strongest phase-advancing cue; increases circadian amplitude and consolidates sleep.

**Evening melanopic suppression** — **Protocol:** Begin dimming lights well before sleep; avoid blue-rich and overhead lighting; minimize close-range screen use late in the evening. **Rationale:** Prevents melatonin suppression and phase delay; preserves biological night.

**True darkness during sleep** — **Protocol:** Sleeping environment maintained near complete darkness; cover LEDs and eliminate ambient light; brief low-intensity red light only if necessary. **Rationale:** Maintains nocturnal melatonin amplitude and hormonal separation of night and day.

**Bright days (amplitude reinforcement)** — **Protocol:** Accumulate substantial daytime brightness, including the morning session; prioritize outdoor exposure. **Rationale:** Circadian robustness depends on contrast between bright days and dark nights.

**Daytime-aligned feeding** — **Protocol:** All caloric intake confined to daytime; consistent first and last meal timing; avoid late-night eating. **Rationale:** Feeding entrains peripheral metabolic clocks and reduces internal desynchrony.

**Exercise earlier in the active phase** — **Protocol:** Schedule exercise in the morning or mid-afternoon; avoid vigorous activity late in the evening. **Rationale:** Exercise acts as a secondary circadian cue; late timing elevates arousal and delays sleep.

**Conflicting zeitgeber elimination** — **Protocol:** Align light, sleep, feeding, and activity schedules consistently across weekdays and weekends. **Rationale:** Misaligned cues fragment internal circadian coordination.

**Night cooling** — **Protocol:** Maintain a cooler sleep environment within comfort limits; optional daytime warmth exposure. **Rationale:** Temperature rhythms support sleep consolidation and circadian signaling.

### 13.2. Tier 2 — Measurement-Supportive Controls

**Caffeine control** — **Protocol:** Fixed early-day cutoff. **Rationale:** Reduces masking of sleep pressure and circadian readouts.

**Alcohol avoidance** — **Protocol:** Avoid during assessment and optimization phases. **Rationale:** Alcohol disrupts sleep architecture and circadian hormone rhythms.

**Social schedule stabilization** — **Protocol:** Minimize late nights and irregular social timing during measurement periods. **Rationale:** Social jetlag introduces phase instability.

### 13.3. Tier 3 — Speculative / Exploratory (Hypothesis-Driven)

**Morning red / near-infrared exposure (Kruse-inspired)** — **Protocol:** Morning exposure to red or near-infrared light via sunlight or standardized device. **Rationale:** Hypothesized mitochondrial redox and nitric-oxide signaling effects.

**Solar-sequence conditioning (Kruse-inspired)** — **Protocol:** Morning outdoor light, optional controlled midday sun exposure appropriate to skin type, strict dark nights. **Rationale:** Hormetic model of environmental entrainment and circadian amplitude enhancement.

**Morning priming and evening downshifting (Zaid-inspired)** — **Protocol:** Combine early light exposure with light movement in the morning; reduce cognitive and sensory load in the evening. **Rationale:** Hypothesized reduction of circadian noise and improved segregation of biological day and night.

**Electromagnetic exposure minimization (Kruse-inspired)** — **Protocol:** Remove phones from the bedroom or use airplane mode; avoid charging devices near bed; optional wired networking at night. **Rationale:** Hypothesized effects of non-native EMF on calcium handling, redox state, and circadian stability.

### 13.4. Zeitgebers

**Primary**

- Light exposure (timing, intensity, spectrum)
- Sleep timing (wake-time anchored)
- Feeding timing
- Physical activity timing
- Temperature (night cooling / day warmth)

**Secondary / masking variables**

- Caffeine
- Alcohol
- Social timing
- Cognitive and sensory load
- Device exposure at night

**Exploratory modifiers**

- Spectral emphasis (red / NIR)
- Solar exposure sequencing
- EMF exposure minimization

### 13.5. Iterative Adjustment

Protocol variables are adjusted one at a time based on:

- phase alignment
- amplitude
- stability
- cross-system coherence

Changes are evaluated after sufficient time for circadian adaptation before further modification.

Protocol adjustments are guided by deviations observed in the [Circadian Tracking Table](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ZSRs-0XkmKa_bJxXlISOKpBUK7OAxhki/edit?gid=1665814829#gid=1665814829), with changes introduced one variable at a time.
