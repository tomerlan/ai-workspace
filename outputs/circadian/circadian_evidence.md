# Evidence
## Empirical Support for the Circadian–Cancer Framework

---

This chapter summarizes the empirical support for the framework, stratified by study type. References are embedded in the text with links to [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Established findings are distinguished from extrapolation where appropriate.

---

## 1. Epidemiological

### Shift work and circadian disruption as carcinogen

Chronic circadian disruption from long-term night shift work, irregular sleep–wake timing, or nocturnal light exposure is consistently associated with increased cancer incidence and worse prognosis.

In 2007, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified shift work that involves circadian disruption as **probably carcinogenic to humans** (Group 2A), based on limited evidence in humans and sufficient evidence from experimental studies [[Straif 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19271347/)]. The classification was reinforced in IARC’s 2020 monograph on night shift work. Large-scale population studies, including increased breast cancer risk in long-term night-shift workers (e.g., nurses), support this classification [[Stevens 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20953253/)]. Considerations for defining shift work in cancer studies emphasize measuring circadian impact rather than hours worked [[IARC Working Group](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20962033/)].

### Rest–activity rhythms and prognosis

Blunted or irregular rest–activity circadian rhythms correlate with reduced survival and accelerated disease progression.

In a US adult population (NHANES), blunted rest–activity rhythm (low relative amplitude) was associated with increased risk of all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality; relative amplitude outperformed most traditional mortality predictors [[Xu 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36450759/)]. Among cancer patients, wearable-measured circadian disruption (low amplitude, low mesor, high fragmentation) predicted increased all-cause, cancer-specific, and cardiovascular mortality and outperformed traditional risk factors [[2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40930750/)].

## 2. Controlled Human Disruption Studies

### Circadian misalignment and DNA repair

Experimental misalignment of behavioral and circadian time induces cancer-relevant molecular changes in humans.

Night-shift schedules cause circadian dysregulation of DNA repair genes and elevated DNA damage in blood [[Cheung 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33638890/)]. Simulated night-shift work induces circadian misalignment of the peripheral blood mononuclear cell transcriptome, with dampened rhythmic gene expression affecting immune and signaling pathways (e.g., natural killer cells, Jun/AP-1, STAT) [[Kervezee 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29735673/)]. Short-term simulated shift work disrupts clock-controlled transcriptional programs related to cell cycle, immune regulation, and metabolism.

## 3. Human Chronotherapy and Rhythm Interventions

### Treatment timing

Administering cancer therapies at circadian-preferred phases can improve tolerability and efficacy.

**Chemotherapy:** A randomized trial of chronomodulated versus constant-rate infusion of oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and folinic acid in metastatic colorectal cancer found chronotherapy reduced severe mucosal toxicity five-fold and increased objective response (51% vs 29%) [[Lévi 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9291901/)]. A meta-analysis showed reduced hematological toxicity, though survival benefits were inconsistent [[Chronomodulated meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37090313/)].

**Immunotherapy:** The LungTIME-C01 phase 3 RCT in 210 treatment-naive NSCLC patients randomized anti-PD-1 immunochemotherapy to early (before 15:00) vs late (after 15:00) for the first four cycles. Early time-of-day administration substantially improved PFS (11.3 vs 5.7 months; HR 0.40) and OS (28.0 vs 16.8 months; HR 0.42). Morning circulating CD8+ T cells increased in the early group and declined in the late group; the activated-to-exhausted CD8+ ratio was higher with early administration [[Huang 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04181-w)].

Drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics are circadian-regulated; host and tumor clock state influence therapeutic window. Benefits vary by chronotype, rhythm stability, drug class, sex, and tumor biology. Phase-referenced scheduling should be personalized to internal circadian markers.

### Structured behavioral rhythms

Interventions that stabilize circadian phase (fixed wake times, timed light exposure, regular activity) improve sleep and quality of life. Observational evidence links rhythm consolidation with longer survival in cancer patients.

## 4. Environmental and Genetic Animal Models

### Environmental disruption

Environmental circadian disruption accelerates tumor growth, impairs immune responses, and increases metastatic potential in rodents.

Light at night induces circadian disruption that accelerates aging and promotes tumorigenesis in rats [[Blask 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23237593/)]. Circadian disruption promotes tumor growth through anabolic host metabolism in rat models [[2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28874144/)]. Chronic jet lag, dim light at night, and rotating light–dark schedules produce similar effects.

### Genetic disruption of clock genes

Genetic disruption of core clock genes modifies tumor initiation and progression, with outcomes depending on gene and context.

Disruption of BMAL1 promotes cancer metastasis through PAI-1–TGF-β–dependent mechanisms [[BMAL1 metastasis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37330661/)]. Clock gene mutations affect aging and carcinogenesis [[Fu 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19805073/)]. Disruption-induced tumors generally show increased stemness, immunosuppression, and metabolic deregulation.

## 5. Reversibility and Circadian Restoration (Preclinical)

### Light structure and melatonin

Restoring light–dark cycles and preserving nocturnal melatonin rhythms can suppress tumor growth and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity in preclinical models. Melatonin’s efficacy depends on preserved circadian amplitude. Melatonin enhances tumor sensitivity to chemo/radiotherapy while protecting normal tissues [[Melatonin review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37916636/)].

### Feeding–fasting structure

Time-restricted feeding aligned with the active phase inhibits tumor growth in rodents.

TRF attenuates mammary tumor growth, reduces metastasis, and normalizes hyperinsulinemia in obesity-enhanced models [[TRF breast](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34894935/)]. TRF attenuates high-fat diet–enhanced spontaneous metastasis of Lewis lung carcinoma [[TRF lung](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30952713/)]. Mechanisms include restored diurnal gene expression in tumors and improved insulin sensitivity.

### Exercise timing

Exercise during the biological active phase improves outcomes when combined with therapy in preclinical models, supporting peripheral clock reinforcement.

### Pharmacologic clock modulation

Small molecules that stabilize or shift core clock components reduce tumor proliferation and reprogram metabolic and immune pathways in experimental systems. Restoration of rhythms reduces dissemination, reestablishes checkpoint timing, and shifts gene expression toward tumor suppression.

## 7. Mechanistic Support from Experimental Systems

Circadian clocks regulate multiple cancer-relevant pathways: cell cycle checkpoints, DNA repair fidelity, apoptotic thresholds, mitochondrial dynamics, metabolic flux, immune gating, adhesion, and angiogenesis. Clock gene manipulation alters tumor behavior by shifting transcriptional control over oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways (e.g., c-MYC, p53, WEE1, mTOR) [[BMAL1/c-MYC](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37330661/)].

Temporal misalignment permits overlap of incompatible processes (e.g., DNA replication during oxidative peaks), increasing error load and tumor permissiveness. Loss of circadian gating reduces precision in stress responses and weakens tumor containment systems at the molecular and systemic level.
