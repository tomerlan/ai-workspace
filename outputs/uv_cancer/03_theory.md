## UV, cancer biology, melanin, vitamin D, and electrophysiology

## UV as a physical input

Ultraviolet, UV, is electromagnetic radiation shorter wavelength than visible light.

- **UVA 315–400 nm**: penetrates deeper in many tissues; couples strongly through redox chemistry and photosensitizers.
- **UVB 280–315 nm**: efficiently drives direct DNA photochemistry; drives vitamin D3 synthesis in skin.
- **UVC 100–280 nm**: largely filtered by the atmosphere; mainly relevant for artificial sources.

 Electromagnetic spectrum overview with wavelength, frequency, and photon energy ranges. Source: Dinksbumf/Inductiveload, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Electromagnetic_spectrum_with_sources.svg`)

![Erythemal action spectrum and UV weighting](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Erythemal_action_spectrum.svg)

Erythemal action spectrum illustrating why erythema-weighted metrics are UVB-biased. Source: Hankwang, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Erythemal_action_spectrum.svg`)

![Sun rays diagram: UVA vs UVB and penetration](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Sun_rays_diagram.svg)

 Schematic showing UVA and UVB bands and qualitative penetration differences. Source: GRPH3B18, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sun_rays_diagram.svg`)

Photon energy scales as \(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\). Shorter wavelength means higher photon energy and different chemistry becomes feasible.

UV exposure is a vector, not a scalar. Biology depends on wavelength mix, dose rate, intermittency, geometry, and host modifiers such as pigmentation, antioxidant capacity, and repair capacity. Public UV indices are often action-spectrum weighted. Erythema, sunburn, is UVB-biased, so low erythema does not imply low UVA.

Penetration is wavelength- and tissue-dependent. UVA penetrates deeper than UVB in skin. Depth delivery and internal exposure are quantitative questions set by spectrum, optical path, and tissue optics.

## UVB to DNA damage and mutation

UVB dose → DNA photoproducts → repair and tolerance → mutation burden → clonal expansion.

### Primary lesions

UVB generates covalent DNA lesions between adjacent pyrimidines, most notably:

- **cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers**, CPDs
- **6–4 photoproducts**, 6–4PP

![Direct DNA damage: UV absorption → cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer (CPD)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3b/Direct_DNA_damage.png)

Direct DNA damage schematic showing UV absorption by a pyrimidine pair and cyclobutane dimer formation (CPD). Source: Gerriet41, Wikimedia Commons, public domain. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Direct_DNA_damage.png`)

CPDs are covalent crosslinks formed between adjacent pyrimidines, most often TT, TC, CT, and CC, after UV excitation. They block replication and transcription, and if they persist into replication, they can be converted into mutations.

![UV-induced DNA mutation schematic](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/DNA_UV_mutation.svg)

UV-induced DNA distortion and mutation schematic. Source: NASA-derived educational figure on Wikimedia Commons, public domain on file page. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:DNA_UV_mutation.svg`)

### Repair and tolerance systems

The main constraint systems are:

- **nucleotide excision repair**, NER, for bulky helix-distorting lesions such as CPDs and 6–4PP
- **base excision repair**, BER, for small oxidative lesions such as 8-oxoG
- **translesion synthesis**, TLS, replication past damage with mutagenic risk

Cooper, PLOS Biology (2006), CC BY 2.5. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nucleotide_Excision_Repair-journal.pbio.0040203.g001.png`)

![DNA repair mechanisms overview](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/DNA_Repair_Mechanisms.png)

 Overview schematic of multiple DNA repair pathways including excision repair. Source: Eunice Laurent, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:DNA_Repair_Mechanisms.png`)

![Base excision repair diagram](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Dna_repair_base_excersion_en.svg)

Base excision repair schematic (BER). Source: LadyofHats, Wikimedia Commons, public domain. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dna_repair_base_excersion_en.svg`)

NER runs as a sequence: damage recognition, local unwinding, dual incision bracketing the lesion, then gap-filling synthesis and ligation. CPDs are often repaired more slowly than 6–4PP, leaving a window where replication can convert lesions into fixed mutations.

### Where “UV becomes carcinogenic”

This route becomes cancer-relevant when lesion formation overlaps with:

- long-lived or repeatedly cycling compartments, so mutations can accumulate
- survival and proliferation signals after injury, so damaged clones expand
- checkpoint and repair limitations, so damaged cells survive with errors
- replication timing and cell-cycle coupling, so lesions persist into S phase and are converted into mutations

![Cell cycle phases including S phase](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Cell_cycle_simple.png)

Cell cycle phases with S phase highlighted as the DNA synthesis window. Source: Simon Caulton, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cell_cycle_simple.png`)

UV mutational signatures are the molecular trace of this chain. Tumor genomes can show enrichment for C→T at dipyrimidines and related patterns when UV damage and imperfect repair were part of tumor history.

### Where melanin fits in this route

Melanin can reduce the effective UVB dose delivered to nuclear DNA by optical attenuation and geometry.

Mechanistic levers:

- **optical attenuation**: melanin absorbs strongly in UVB; more melanin between the incident photons and nuclear DNA reduces the photon flux that reaches DNA, lowering cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer (CPD) formation rate for a fixed external dose.
- **scattering and path length**: melanosomes and the melanin granule field can scatter photons, changing local optical path and redistributing dose in micro-geometry; the sign is usually protective for nuclear UVB exposure when melanin is positioned above nuclei.
- **supranuclear caps**: melanosomes transferred to keratinocytes can localize above the nucleus, forming a geometric “umbrella” that reduces UV reaching nuclear DNA even if the bulk cytosol is otherwise similar.
- **melanosome architecture**: protection depends on melanosome size and number, dispersion versus aggregation, persistence through epidermal layers, and eumelanin versus pheomelanin chemistry.

Cell biology of how the geometry is achieved:

- melanocytes synthesize melanin inside melanosomes, transport melanosomes along dendrites, and transfer melanosomes to keratinocytes.
- keratinocytes then position melanosomes (including supranuclear cap formation) and carry them upward through the epidermis; retention and distribution across layers determine how much melanin lies on the dominant optical path to basal nuclei.

![Melanocyte dendrites and melanosome distribution in epidermis (schematic)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Schematic_illustration_of_melanocyte_structure.png)

Schematic illustration of melanocyte structure and melanin granule content in the human epidermis, emphasizing dendritic geometry relevant to melanosome transfer and supranuclear shielding. Source: Mazur/Kwiatkowska/Reich, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Schematic_illustration_of_melanocyte_structure.png`)

Pigmentation is not only pigment quantity. Pigmentation biology couples to signaling programs that modulate repair capacity. **melanocortin 1 receptor**, MC1R, signaling through cyclic adenosine monophosphate, cAMP, pathways in melanocytes can augment repair kinetics and antioxidant programs. MC1R is engaged by **α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone**, α-MSH, and **adrenocorticotropic hormone**, ACTH.

### Pigment chemistry context

Pigment quantity and pigment chemistry are separable.

- **eumelanin** is brown-black and is typically more photostable and more protective
- **pheomelanin** is red-yellow and sulfur-containing and can be more photounstable and pro-oxidant under UVA in some contexts

Mechanistic implications of that chemistry distinction:

- **photostability**: eumelanin tends to dissipate absorbed energy with less propagation into reactive intermediates; pheomelanin can more readily participate in redox cycling and reactive oxygen species generation under UVA-rich conditions.
- **redox buffering and metal binding**: melanin can bind transition metals and host stable radical character; this can buffer oxidative events, but it can also create context-dependent photochemistry (metal-dependent radical propagation).
- **UVB vs UVA split**: the “melanin protects DNA from UVB CPDs” statement is primarily an optical/geometry statement; UVA introduces additional chemistry routes (oxidative damage and melanin-mediated delayed CPDs) that can partially decouple “more pigment” from “less total DNA damage.”

Delayed damage caveat (important for sign thinking):

- melanin can participate in delayed CPD formation after UVA exposure via chemiexcitation (“dark CPDs”), so melanin is not a pure one-way shield under all spectra: [Premi et al., Chemiexcitation of melanin derivatives induces DNA photoproducts long after UV exposure (Science, 2015; full text)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4432913/)

Melanin synthesis, simplified:

- tyrosine → L-DOPA → DOPAquinone (tyrosinase)
- branching:
  - low cysteine availability biases toward eumelanin-like polymers
  - high cysteine availability biases toward pheomelanin-like polymers

## UVA to oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue remodeling

UVA dose → reactive oxygen species → lipid peroxidation and protein oxidation → inflammatory signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling → selection pressures that can promote progression.

### ROS generation and propagation

UVA often acts through excited-state chromophores, endogenous or exogenous, generating singlet oxygen, superoxide, and hydrogen peroxide; triggering lipid peroxidation and reactive aldehydes; and oxidizing proteins and nucleic acids. UVB can also contribute via inflammation and secondary reactive oxygen species.

![Active oxygen species](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Active_oxygen_species.svg)

Lewis structures of common reactive oxygen species relevant to UVA photochemistry. Source: Calvero, Wikimedia Commons, public domain. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Active_oxygen_species.svg`)

Lipid peroxidation is a damage amplifier. Reactive aldehydes can diffuse beyond the original absorption site and form adducts with proteins and DNA, spreading injury across membranes and organelles.

![Lipid peroxidation radical chain reaction](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Lipid_peroxidation.svg)

Lipid peroxidation radical chain reaction schematic (initiation, propagation, termination). Source: Tim Vickers/Fvasconcellos, Wikimedia Commons, public domain. (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lipid_peroxidation.svg`)

### Redox state as a control variable

Redox state is the balance between electron donors and acceptors and the cell’s ability to buffer oxidative chemistry. In practice it is proxied by coupled pools such as NAD⁺/NADH, NADP⁺/NADPH, glutathione disulfide/glutathione (GSSG/GSH), and thioredoxin systems. It matters because many signaling and checkpoint circuits are redox-sensitive.

In cancer-relevant terms, a redox shift can reweight:

- nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells, NF-κB, stress and inflammatory programs
- mitogen-activated protein kinase, MAPK, growth and stress kinase cascades
- tumor protein p53, p53, damage checkpoints and apoptosis thresholds
- mitochondrial versus glycolytic flux and apoptotic susceptibility

### Photoimmunology and promotion pressure

UV exposure reshapes immunity at multiple layers. Damage-associated molecular patterns plus cytokines plus altered antigen presentation can shift tumor surveillance pressure and tumor–immune equilibrium. Cancer behavior is not only mutation; it is immune recognition versus escape, inflammation-driven growth signals, and microenvironment remodeling.

### Where melanin fits in this route

Melanin modifies oxidative chemistry by buffering or amplifying it depending on context.

- **eumelanin** tends to be more photostable and more protective
- **pheomelanin** can be more photounstable and more pro-oxidant under UVA in some contexts

Melanin binds metals, notably iron and copper. That can be protective by reducing free metal-catalyzed radical formation, but under some conditions it can create pro-oxidant microenvironments through Fenton-like chemistry when antioxidants are depleted.

Delayed chemistry matters here. Some mechanistic models propose that UV exposure can initiate chemistry that continues producing DNA lesions after irradiation stops, via excited intermediates generated in melanogenesis and redox chemistry. If true, damage is not only during exposure, but also during a recovery window shaped by antioxidant availability and inflammation state.

## UVB to vitamin D signaling and immune surveillance

UVB dose → vitamin D3 synthesis in skin → circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D availability → local activation and vitamin D receptor signaling → immune and tumor programs that can shift progression and therapy response.

### Metabolites and what they mean

- **vitamin D3** is produced in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol under UVB
- vitamin D3 is hydroxylated in liver to **25-hydroxyvitamin D**, 25(OH)D, the main status marker
- 25(OH)D is activated in kidney and also in other tissues to **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D**, 1,25(OH)₂D, the active hormone form

25(OH)D is a relatively stable pool. 1,25(OH)₂D is tightly regulated and is not a simple “more is better” status marker.

Vitamin D2 and D3 are distinct inputs. Human physiology is largely framed around D3-like biology, but measurements and supplementation can involve both forms.

### Intracrine vitamin D signaling

Many immune and non-immune tissues locally convert 25(OH)D to active forms. Vitamin D signaling is therefore an availability plus activation problem. Serum 25(OH)D can be adequate while local activation is limited, and vice versa.

### Immune and tumor cell programs

Vitamin D receptor, VDR, signaling can shift antigen presentation state, T cell polarization, innate effector programs, and inflammatory set points. The direction is context-dependent because tumor control depends on immune baseline, tumor immunogenicity, and microenvironment constraints.

VDR-linked regulation is also discussed through differentiation programs, cell-cycle checkpoints, and apoptosis susceptibility in tumor cells and premalignant tissues.

### Why supplementation can diverge from sunlight

Supplementation changes vitamin D metabolites without reproducing UVA-weighted pathways, melanin photophysics, or the geometry and timing that come with light exposure. Observational 25(OH)D is a composite of sun exposure, diet, adiposity, inflammation, and behavior.

### Why different study designs disagree

- **observational**: serum 25(OH)D partially reflects overall health and outdoor behavior and can be confounded by inflammation, adiposity, and reverse causation
- **randomized supplementation**: moves 25(OH)D without recreating UVA-linked mediator routes or exposure timing
- **Mendelian randomization**: instruments represent lifelong differences and implicitly assume specific causal structures; it probes a different perturbation than either sunlight or short-term pills

## UVA to nitric oxide and other skin-derived mediators

UVA dose → mobilization of nitric-oxide–related species → vascular tone and perfusion shifts → immune trafficking and inflammatory tone shifts → indirect effects on tumor control.

### Nitric oxide photobiology

UVA can mobilize nitric-oxide–related species through photolysis of nitrite and nitrate pools and nitrosothiols. Downstream effects are vascular tone, perfusion, and signaling shifts. Any cancer relevance is indirect through systemic physiology, immune trafficking, and inflammation.

### Other mediator channels

Skin is an active neuroimmune and endocrine signaling organ. UV can influence local production of peptides and cytokines that have systemic reach. These mediator routes provide plausible non–vitamin D coupling paths for sunlight-like exposures.

## UVA and visible to melanin transduction and electrophysiology

Minimal causal chain (UVA small amplitude → melanin → water structure → cancer-compatible / cancer-incompatible):

UVA/visible dose (sub-erythemal) → melanin photophysics (redox / protonation / surface charge) → interfacial water polarization + proton mobility (nanometer-scale) → local ion adsorption/activity (K+, Na+, H+, Ca2+) and pHi microdomains → switch-like changes in membrane-adjacent protein–water state (association–induction style R↔A transitions) → shifts in electrical/metabolic set points (Vmem, pHi, ΔΨm, Ca2+ dynamics) → phenotype bias.

Definitions used below:

- **Vmem**: plasma membrane potential (the electrical potential difference recorded between bulk cytosol and bulk extracellular fluid).
- **pHi**: intracellular pH.
- **ΔΨm**: mitochondrial membrane potential.
- **pHi microdomains**: localized differences in pHi near membranes/organelles. Bulk cytosolic pH can look stable while near-surface pH differs enough to change gating, transporter directionality, and enzyme rates.
- **R↔A transition (association–induction framing)**: a switch in local protein–water–ion state between a more ordered, strongly associated/bound-water state (“resting”) and a more labile, less-associated state (“active”), with downstream consequences for ion adsorption, water binding, and electrical set points.

Scope constraint (what cell types this can apply to directly):

- This route requires melanin (typically packaged in melanosomes, or as tissue-specific melanins) to be physically present at relevant interfaces. It therefore applies most directly to **melanocytes** and to **keratinocytes that have received melanin** from melanocytes in pigmented skin.
- Other physiological melanin compartments exist but are anatomically specific: **ocular melanin** in the retinal pigment epithelium and uveal tract, and **neuromelanin** in subsets of catecholaminergic neurons. These sites differ in optics and disease context and are not general proxies for “all internal tissues contain melanin.”
- For most internal cancers (non-melanocytic tumors), endogenous melanin is not present. Extending a “melanin transducer” mechanism to those settings would require **delivering melanin or a melanin-like interface** to tumor tissue (or identifying a different endogenous chromophore with analogous transduction properties). Any such extension is speculative and must be evaluated with action-spectrum and localization tests.

### Melanin context beyond “sunscreen pigment”

Melanin is a chemically active polymer system that:

- absorbs and dissipates UV and visible energy
- binds metals
- participates in redox buffering

Melanin-like pigments exist outside skin in eye and brain. These sites differ in optics, microenvironment, and disease context, so “melanin exists outside skin” does not imply a single universal cancer role, but it does matter for any claim that melanin is only a skin sunscreen.

### Why melanin is singled out for transduction claims

Melanin combines properties that rarely co-occur in biology:

- broadband absorption from UV into visible and near-infrared
- persistent radical character and redox-buffer behavior
- hydration-dependent charge transport in disordered polymer models
- large surface area packaged into melanosomes, creating mesoscale interfaces
- spatial localization and geometry control through melanosome distribution

These properties concentrate photon absorption into a hydrated interface with charge and proton degrees of freedom, which is the required starting point for any light-to-electrophysiology coupling.

### Melanin as a mixed ionic–electronic conductor

Semiconductor-like behavior has been proposed for melanin in the specific sense that hydrated melanin can support coupled charge transport with both:

- **electronic carriers** associated with redox states and radical character in the polymer
- **ionic and protonic carriers** in the hydration layer and bound counterion environment

In that picture, effective conductivity and charge storage depend strongly on hydration, protonation state, ionic strength, and counterion composition. A mechanistic claim of “melanin as semiconductor” therefore reduces to measurable dependencies: illumination changes melanin redox and protonation state, which changes interfacial potential and local ion activity at the melanosome boundary.

### Association–induction coupling at interfaces

Association–induction treats the cell as a coupled hydrated gel rather than a perfectly well-mixed solution. The useful move is to treat **protein–water–ion assemblies** as stateful materials whose interfacial charge and hydration can switch binding preferences and electrical set points.

Measurable interface-level variables:

- interfacial or bound water fraction near surfaces
- ion binding and activity shifts near protein and polymer surfaces, especially potassium versus sodium partitioning
- effective dielectric environment near surfaces
- proton mobility and buffering near interfaces
- ion activity versus concentration
- local electrostatic potentials near surfaces

### Stepwise coupling chain

For this route to change whole-cell behavior, the perturbation has to cross three ports cleanly.

- **Port A (photon → melanin state variable)**: absorption changes melanin’s redox/protonation/surface charge state.
- **Port B (melanin → water/ion microenvironment)**: that state change reweights interfacial water polarization, local dielectric screening, proton availability, and ion activities near the relevant interfaces.
- **Port C (microenvironment → whole-cell set points)**: those local changes propagate into a sustained shift in Vmem / pHi / ΔΨm / Ca2+ dynamics rather than averaging away.

#### Port A: UVA/visible absorption → melanin redox/proton/surface-charge shift

Why UVA and visible:

- melanin absorbs strongly across UVA and visible wavelengths
- UVA penetrates deeper than UVB in skin and is therefore more plausible for effects that do not rely on UVB photoproduct chemistry

What “small amplitude UVA” means mechanistically:

- **non-thermal**: the relevant state variables are charge/proton/redox, not bulk heating
- **near-threshold**: if the coupled protein–water–ion system sits near a cooperative transition, a small, repeated bias can produce a discrete state shift (a local R↔A transition) instead of a proportional response

Concrete state variables that can, in principle, change in melanin on illumination:

- redox distribution and persistent radical fraction in the polymer
- protonation state at functional groups; proton-coupled electron transfer–style coupling between redox and proton state
- effective surface charge density at the hydrated interface (equivalently, surface potential)

#### Port B: melanin surface state → interfacial water + ion activity + pHi microdomains

Electrostatic coupling in electrolyte is screened. At physiological ionic strength, the Debye length is on the order of ~1 nm. That pushes the mechanism toward **near-surface microenvironments** and away from any expectation of a long-range electric field reaching across the cell.

The mechanism then becomes local but still biologically potent because channels, transporters, and enzymes operate in that near-field regime:

- **interfacial water polarization** changes effective dielectric screening and thus the strength of ion–oxygen interactions at nearby oxo-groups (carboxyls and carbonyls)
- **proton mobility and buffering** at interfaces can shift pHi microdomains without obvious bulk pH changes
- **ion activity vs concentration**: the relevant control variable for gating and binding is local activity (effective chemical potential), which can change even when bulk concentrations barely move

Association–induction translation (what “water structure” is doing here):

- interface charge/hydration changes reweight electron density at oxo-groups (a c-value / c-value-analogue type change)
- that can switch which counterions are preferentially associated (K+-like vs Na+/H+-like)
- the switch changes local water binding (more bound/polarized vs more bulk-like) and local osmotic/electrostatic loads

#### Port C: microdomains → sustained shifts in Vmem / pHi / ΔΨm (less pump-centered framing)

Two equivalent ways to describe the same measurement:

- **Permeability parameterization (mainstream electrophysiology)**: Vmem is the steady-state voltage that satisfies current balance across the membrane capacitance given the available conductances. Microdomain pH/surface-potential changes matter only if they change the state of conductances that dominate current balance.
- **Adsorption parameterization (association–induction / CCSA framing)**: Vmem is treated as a surface adsorption potential arising from fixed charges plus bound counterions at interfaces. In that framing, pumps and channels are **effectors** that redistribute ions and water to satisfy electroneutrality and stabilize a state, rather than primary causes of the set point.

In both parameterizations, the practical requirement is the same: you must identify which membrane-adjacent proteins or interfaces act as the rate-limiting controller for the set point shift.

Ports that can convert a nanometer-scale change into a cell-scale state change:

- **pH-sensitive conductances and exchangers**: pHi microdomains can shift gating thresholds and transporter directionality
- **Ca2+ as an amplifier**: small shifts in Vmem or surface potential can change Ca2+ entry/driving force; Ca2+ can then reprogram conductances and gene programs
- **ATP/ADP coupling**: if the perturbation shifts ATP binding state in a local matrix domain, it can bias a local R↔A transition and thereby change K+/Na+ association and water binding in that domain

5) **Mitochondrial coupling**

ΔΨm is generated by the electron transport chain pumping protons to create a proton motive force. ΔΨm is sensitive to cytosolic ADP and ATP, Ca2+ handling, intracellular pH, buffering state, and redox state. If a light→melanin process shifts Vmem, pHi, or Ca2+ handling, it can shift ΔΨm and metabolic set points.

6) **Phenotype bias**

Bioelectric control frameworks treat Vmem as a state variable correlated with proliferation versus differentiation. Many cancers show ion channel remodeling and altered pH control. This axis becomes cancer-relevant when the perturbation is sustained enough to bias those set points.

Two sign regimes are compatible with the upstream chain; the sign is set by which ports dominate:

- **Cancer-compatible direction (often but not always depolarizing)**:
  - Vmem shifts toward more depolarized set points
  - pHi shifts upward (more alkaline cytosol) and/or pH gradients sharpen at membranes
  - Ca2+ entry/oscillations become more permissive
  - ΔΨm and redox state shift toward a proliferative metabolic regime
- **Cancer-incompatible direction (often but not always hyperpolarizing or stress-stabilizing)**:
  - Vmem shifts toward more hyperpolarized, differentiation-biased set points
  - pHi regulation shifts toward lower pHi or reduced pH gradient support for growth
  - Ca2+ entry is constrained or reprogrammed toward differentiation/apoptosis-like signaling
  - ΔΨm shifts toward lower proliferative drive or activates growth-arrest programs

### Necessary conditions for a whole-cell effect

- the interface perturbation is large enough and long-lived enough to outcompete buffering and diffusion
- it couples to the interface/protein states that actually set whole-cell Vmem, not only to perinuclear microdomains
- the action spectrum tracks melanin-weighted absorption, not erythema weighting

## UVB to DNA damage and mutation

UVB dose → DNA photoproducts → repair and tolerance → mutation burden → clonal expansion.

UVB generates covalent DNA lesions between adjacent pyrimidines, most notably **cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers**, CPDs, and **6–4 photoproducts**, 6–4PP. These distort the DNA helix, block replication and transcription, and create characteristic mutation patterns when repaired imperfectly.

The main constraint systems are:

- **Nucleotide excision repair**, NER, for bulky helix-distorting lesions such as CPDs and 6–4PP
- **Base excision repair**, BER, for small oxidative lesions such as 8-oxoG
- **Translesion synthesis**, TLS, replication past damage with mutagenic risk

NER runs as a sequence: damage recognition, local unwinding, dual incision bracketing the lesion, then gap-filling synthesis and ligation. CPDs are often repaired more slowly than 6–4PP, leaving a larger window where replication can convert lesions into mutations.

UV mutational signatures are the molecular trace of this chain. Tumor genomes can show enrichment for C→T at dipyrimidines and related patterns when UV damage and imperfect repair were part of tumor history.

Melanin modifies this route by reducing effective UVB delivery to nuclear DNA. Optical attenuation and geometry matter. Melanosomes transferred to keratinocytes can localize above the nucleus as supranuclear caps, reducing UV reaching nuclear DNA. Protection depends on melanosome size and number, distribution, persistence through the epidermis, and eumelanin versus pheomelanin chemistry.

Pigmentation is not only pigment quantity. Pigmentation biology couples to signaling programs that modulate repair capacity. MC1R signaling through cAMP pathways can augment repair kinetics in melanocytes, separating pigment amount from repair state.

## UVA to oxidative stress and tissue remodeling

UVA dose → reactive oxygen species → lipid peroxidation and protein oxidation → inflammatory signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling → selection pressures that can promote progression.

UVA often acts through excited-state chromophores, endogenous or exogenous, generating singlet oxygen, superoxide, and hydrogen peroxide; triggering lipid peroxidation and reactive aldehydes; and oxidizing proteins and nucleic acids. UVB can also contribute via inflammation and secondary ROS.

Lipid peroxidation is a damage amplifier. Reactive aldehydes can diffuse beyond the original photon absorption site and form adducts with proteins and DNA, spreading injury across membranes and organelles.

Redox state modulates signaling networks such as NF-κB, MAPK, and p53 responses, apoptosis versus survival decisions, immune activation versus exhaustion, and metabolic reprogramming between mitochondrial and glycolytic flux. Cancer-relevant risk can arise when oxidative stress is high enough to increase mutational and inflammatory noise but not high enough to kill damaged cells, creating a selection environment favorable to malignant evolution.

UV exposure reshapes immune tone. Damage-associated molecular patterns plus cytokines plus altered antigen presentation can shift tumor surveillance pressure and tumor–immune equilibrium. Cancer behavior is not only mutation; it is immune recognition versus escape, inflammation-driven growth signals, and microenvironment remodeling.

Melanin modifies this route by buffering or amplifying oxidative chemistry depending on context. Eumelanin tends to be more photostable and more protective. Pheomelanin can be more photounstable and more pro-oxidant under UVA in some contexts. Melanin binds metals, notably iron and copper. That can be protective by reducing free metal-catalyzed radical formation, but under some conditions it can create pro-oxidant microenvironments through Fenton-like chemistry when antioxidants are depleted.

Delayed chemistry matters here. Melanin-linked excited intermediates can extend damage into the recovery window after irradiation, changing the naive model from damage only during exposure to damage during exposure plus damage during recovery.

## UVB to vitamin D signaling and immune surveillance

UVB dose → vitamin D3 synthesis in skin → circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D availability → local activation and vitamin D receptor signaling → immune and tumor programs that can shift progression and therapy response.

UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D3 in skin, then to vitamin D3. Vitamin D3 is hydroxylated in liver to **25-hydroxyvitamin D**, 25(OH)D, and then activated in kidney and in other tissues to **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D**, 1,25(OH)₂D. 25(OH)D is the main status marker. 1,25(OH)₂D is tightly regulated and is not a simple status marker.

Many immune and non-immune tissues locally convert 25(OH)D to active forms. Vitamin D signaling is an availability plus activation problem, not only a serum concentration problem.

Vitamin D receptor signaling can shift antigen presentation state, T cell polarization, innate effector programs, and inflammatory set points. The direction is context-dependent because tumor control depends on immune baseline, tumor immunogenicity, and microenvironment constraints.

VDR-linked regulation is also discussed through differentiation programs, cell-cycle checkpoints, and apoptosis susceptibility. Whether this dominates in humans depends on tissue context, dosing, baseline deficiency, and duration.

Supplementation changes vitamin D metabolites without reproducing UVA-weighted pathways, melanin photophysics, or the exposure geometry and timing that come with light. Observational 25(OH)D is a composite of sun exposure, diet, adiposity, inflammation, and behavior.

## UVA to nitric oxide and other mediators

UVA dose → mobilization of nitric-oxide–related species → vascular tone and perfusion shifts → immune trafficking and inflammatory tone shifts → indirect effects on tumor control.

UVA can mobilize nitric-oxide–related species through photolysis of nitrite and nitrate pools and nitrosothiols. Downstream effects are vascular tone, perfusion, and signaling shifts. Any cancer relevance is indirect through systemic physiology, immune trafficking, and inflammation, and is therefore highly sensitive to confounding when read from human mortality associations.

Skin is an active neuroimmune and endocrine signaling organ. UV can influence local production of peptides and cytokines that have systemic reach. These mediator routes provide plausible non–vitamin D coupling paths for sunlight-like exposures.

## UVA and visible to melanin transduction and electrophysiology

UVA and visible dose → melanin state in melanosomes → interfacial electrostatics and pH microdomains → channel and pump behavior → control variables \(V_m\), \(pH_i\), \(\Delta\Psi_m\) → proliferation and differentiation bias.

This route treats melanin as a hydrated, redox-active polymer interface that can couple photon absorption to charge and proton state variables. The claim is not that bulk cytosolic concentrations change. The claim is that microdomains near charged interfaces change, and channels and pumps operate in that near-field regime.

Melanin is singled out because it combines broadband absorption, persistent radical and redox buffering, hydration-dependent charge transport in disordered polymer models, large surface area packaged into melanosomes, and spatial localization through melanosome distribution. These properties do not prove the route. They make it mechanically specific.

Interfacial water enters as measurable physics: local electrostatic potentials near surfaces, effective dielectric environment, proton mobility and buffering, ion activity versus concentration, and the fraction of water strongly associated with macromolecular surfaces. Channels and enzymes respond to local activity and local potential, not only bulk concentration.

An association–induction framing treats the cell as a coupled hydrated gel rather than a perfectly well-mixed solution. Interface-level perturbations can then propagate into cell-scale electrical set points.

The coupling has to pass through concrete intermediates:

- Illumination changes melanin charge, protonation, and redox state at the melanosome interface
- Local ion activity and local pH change near membranes
- Channel gating thresholds, transporter directionality, and pump load shift
- Current balance shifts whole-cell membrane potential \(V_m\)

Direction is not implied. A depolarizing shift typically corresponds to increased inward Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ conductance, decreased outward K⁺ conductance, altered Cl⁻ handling, and changes in electrogenic pump currents. The sign is determined by which conductances change and on what timescale.

Mitochondrial membrane potential \(\Delta\Psi_m\) is generated by the electron transport chain pumping protons to create a proton motive force. \(\Delta\Psi_m\) is sensitive to cytosolic ADP and ATP, Ca²⁺ handling, intracellular pH, buffering state, and redox state. If a light to melanin process alters \(V_m\), \(pH_i\), or Ca²⁺ handling, it can shift \(\Delta\Psi_m\) and metabolic set points.

Bioelectric control frameworks treat \(V_m\) as a state variable correlated with proliferation versus differentiation. Many cancers show ion channel remodeling and altered pH control. This route targets those state variables directly.

### Quantitative scaffold

Let \(I_{\lambda}(z)\) be irradiance at depth \(z\) for wavelength \(\lambda\).

$$
I_{\lambda}(z) \approx I_{\lambda,0}\,\exp\big(-\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\,z\big)
$$

Absorbed power in melanin:

$$
P_{\mathrm{abs}}^{\mathrm{mel}}(z) \approx \int I_{\lambda}(z)\,\mu_{a}^{\mathrm{mel}}(\lambda)\,d\lambda
$$

Photon absorption rate:

$$
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{abs}}(z) \approx \int \frac{I_{\lambda}(z)\,\mu_{a}^{\mathrm{mel}}(\lambda)}{hc/\lambda}\,d\lambda
$$

Absorption must map to a charge or proton state variable:

$$
\dot{\sigma} = q\,\phi\,\dot{n}_{\mathrm{abs}} - \frac{\sigma}{\tau}
$$

\(\sigma\) is an effective surface charge density or equivalent interface state variable. \(\phi\) is charges or protons moved per absorbed photon. \(\tau\) is a relaxation time.

Electrostatic coupling is screened. The Debye length:

$$
\lambda_D \sim \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\varepsilon_0 kT}{2 N_A e^2 I}}
$$

Ionic strength \(I\) sets screening. Long-range fields decay rapidly, so the effect has to live in microdomains or propagate through global modulators such as \(pH_i\) and Ca²⁺.

At the electrophysiology level:

$$
\sum_i I_i(V_m) = 0
$$

and a perturbation gives:

$$
\Delta V_m \approx - \frac{\sum_i \Delta I_i}{\sum_i \left(\partial I_i/\partial V_m\right)}
$$

A photon-driven interface effect changes \(V_m\) only if it produces a nontrivial \(\sum_i \Delta I_i\) through identifiable channels and pumps.

- Mediation: blocking specific conductances, transporters, or pumps removes the \(V_m\), \(pH_i\), or \(\Delta\Psi_m\) shift without changing irradiation.

## UV as a physical input

Ultraviolet, UV, is electromagnetic radiation shorter wavelength than visible light. Three bands matter.

- **UVA 315–400 nm** penetrates deeper in skin and often couples through photosensitizers and redox chemistry.
- **UVB 280–315 nm** is more efficient at direct DNA photoproduct formation and also drives vitamin D3 synthesis in skin.
- **UVC 100–280 nm** is largely filtered by the atmosphere and is mainly relevant for artificial sources.

Wavelength matters because photon energy scales as \(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\): shorter wavelength means higher photon energy and different chemistry becomes feasible.

UV dose is not one number. What biology experiences depends on wavelength mix, UVA versus UVB, dose rate and intermittency, gradual versus burning, exposure geometry, and host modifiers such as pigmentation, antioxidant capacity, and repair capacity.

Most public UV metrics are action-spectrum weighted. The key example is erythema, sunburn: erythema weighting is strongly UVB-biased, so a low erythema day can still contain substantial UVA. That matters because many proposed systemic and melanin-coupled pathways are UVA and visible weighted.

## How UV is known to interact with biology

UV can couple into biology through multiple routes at once. Some routes are clearly injury-like, such as DNA photoproduct formation and inflammatory signaling. Other routes can act like regulatory inputs, such as mediator release, endocrine metabolites, and melanin-linked photophysics.

Penetration is wavelength- and tissue-dependent. UVA penetrates deeper than UVB in skin. Under some geometries and in some tissues, a nontrivial photon flux can reach deeper compartments. If deep penetration is relevant for a given claim, it has to be treated as a quantitative question about spectrum and optical path, not as an assumption.

Two UV-linked systemic couplings are well-characterized. **UVB drives vitamin D**: UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol into vitamin D3 in skin, then metabolism produces circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D and active hormone forms that act through the vitamin D receptor in immune and tissue programs. **UVA can mobilize redox mediators**: UVA penetrates into dermis and near-vascular compartments and can mobilize nitric-oxide–related species stored in skin, with systemic vascular and signaling consequences.

Melanin sits at the interface of damage and signal. It attenuates photon flux to sensitive targets and shifts local chemistry through redox buffering, metal binding, and delayed chemistry after irradiation. It also exists outside skin in eye and brain, which matters for any claim that melanin is only a sunscreen pigment.

## Routes from UV to cancer biology

UV can connect to cancer biology through multiple routes that can run in parallel. Each route has a different action spectrum, different dependence on dose pattern, and different failure modes.

### Route 1: UVB to DNA damage to initiation
UVB dose → CPDs and 6–4PP → repair and tolerance → mutation burden → clonal expansion → cancer initiation. This route is strongest where the irradiated tissue contains proliferative compartments over long time spans.

### Route 2: UVA to oxidative chemistry to promotion and progression
UVA dose → reactive oxygen species and lipid peroxidation → protein and DNA oxidation, inflammatory signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling → shifts in microenvironment and selection pressures that can promote progression. This route can also couple back into mutation through oxidative lesions.

### Route 3: UVB to vitamin D axis to immune surveillance and tumor programs
UVB dose → vitamin D3 synthesis in skin → circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D availability → local activation and vitamin D receptor signaling → shifts in immune programs and tumor cell differentiation checkpoints. This route can act on progression and therapy response without requiring UV to reach a tumor directly.

### Route 4: UVA to nitric oxide mobilization to systemic physiology
UVA dose → mobilization of nitric-oxide–related species stored in skin → vascular tone and perfusion shifts, immune trafficking and signaling changes → indirect effects on inflammation and tumor control. The cancer relevance, if any, is indirect and can be endpoint-dependent.

### Route 5: UVA and visible to melanin to electrophysiology
UVA and visible dose → melanin in melanosomes, charge, proton, and redox state → interfacial electrostatics and pH microdomains → channel and pump behavior → control variables \(V_m\), \(pH_i\), \(\Delta\Psi_m\) → proliferation and differentiation bias. This route is the core association–induction and semiconductor-like coupling idea developed later in this page.

## DNA damage and repair

### Direct photoproducts
UVB can generate covalent lesions between adjacent pyrimidines, most notably **cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers**, CPDs, and **6–4 photoproducts**, 6–4PP. These distort the DNA helix, block replication and transcription, increase misincorporation if bypassed, and create characteristic mutation patterns when repaired imperfectly.


#### What CPDs are and why they matter
**CPDs** are covalent crosslinks formed between adjacent pyrimidines, most often TT, TC, CT, and CC, after UV excitation. They matter because:
- they are frequent UV lesions,
- they can persist if repair is slow or overwhelmed,
- and they are a mechanistic bridge from “UV exposure” to “mutations” in proliferative cells.

### Repair systems
Major pathways:
- **Nucleotide excision repair**, NER: central for bulky helix-distorting lesions such as CPDs and 6–4PP.
- **Base excision repair**, BER: central for small oxidative lesions, for example 8-oxoG.
- **Translesion synthesis**, TLS: allows replication past damage but can be mutagenic.

High-level implication: cancer risk can be influenced by anything that shifts lesion formation rate, repair capacity or timing, tolerance/bypass behavior, or replication timing relative to damage load.

### Why CPDs can persist
Nucleotide excision repair (NER) runs as a sequence: damage recognition (global-genome vs transcription-coupled), local unwinding, dual incision bracketing the lesion, then gap-filling synthesis and ligation.

CPDs can be repaired more slowly than 6-4PPs in many contexts, creating a time window where replication and transcription can encounter lesions.

### UV mutational signatures
UV exposure can produce characteristic tumor mutational signatures (classically enriched for C→T at dipyrimidines). These signatures help link UV damage processes to tumor genomes when present.

### Replication timing and cell-cycle coupling
UV damage becomes carcinogenic when damage lands in proliferative compartments, repair/tolerance is imperfect, and checkpoints allow survival with mutations.

This matters because:
- basal keratinocytes and melanocyte lineage cells sit at the interface of UV exposure and long-lived mutation accumulation,
- inflammation and growth signals after injury can increase proliferation during a window of elevated damage.

## Oxidative stress and redox biology

### ROS generation pathways
UVA often acts through excited-state chromophores, endogenous or exogenous, generating singlet oxygen, superoxide, and hydrogen peroxide; triggering lipid peroxidation and reactive aldehydes; and oxidizing proteins and nucleic acids.

UVB can also contribute to oxidative stress via inflammation and secondary ROS.

### Why redox matters for cancer
Redox state modulates:
- DNA damage burden (oxidative lesions),
- signaling networks (NF-κB, MAPK, p53 responses),
- apoptosis vs survival decisions,
- immune cell activation and exhaustion,
- metabolic reprogramming (mitochondrial vs glycolytic flux).

Conceptual risk: systems can shift into a regime where oxidative stress is high enough to increase mutational and inflammatory noise, but not high enough to kill damaged cells, creating a selection environment favorable to malignant evolution.

### Lipid peroxidation as a “damage amplifier”
UVA-driven lipid peroxidation generates reactive aldehydes and secondary electrophiles that:
- diffuse beyond the initial photon absorption site,
- form adducts with proteins and DNA,
- and can propagate damage across membranes and organelles.

## Inflammation and photoimmunology

UV exposure can reshape immunity at multiple layers: local skin immunity (keratinocyte cytokines, antigen-presenting cell behavior, melanocyte signaling) and systemic immune tone (circulating cytokines, regulatory T cell activity, antigen presentation dynamics).

A minimal mechanistic framing:
UV → damage signals (damage-associated molecular patterns, DAMPs) + cytokines + altered antigen presentation → immunomodulation (which can include suppression of some responses) → altered tumor surveillance and tumor–immune equilibrium.

This matters because cancer is not only a mutation problem; it is also a problem of immune recognition vs escape, inflammation-driven growth signals, and microenvironment remodeling.

### Core immune mediators frequently implicated
Common immunologic “handles” in UV literature include:
- **IL-10** and other immunoregulatory cytokines (shifting toward tolerogenic states),
- altered antigen-presenting cell function and migration,
- expansion of regulatory T cell activity,
- changes in local innate immune responses after epithelial injury.

## Melanin and pigmentation biology

Melanin is not only a “shield.” It is a chemically active polymer system that:
- absorbs and dissipates UV/visible energy,
- binds metals,
- participates in redox buffering,
- and changes the microenvironment that determines whether UV produces net damage or net dissipation.

### How melanin protects skin

Melanin protection has multiple layers that can be separated conceptually.

#### Optical attenuation
- **Absorption**: melanin is a broadband absorber; it reduces photon flux reaching sensitive targets.
- **Dissipation**: absorbed energy is mostly dissipated non-radiatively (heat), lowering excited-state chemistry in other chromophores.
- **Scattering and geometry**: distribution of melanosomes changes the optical path and local photon density.

#### Spatial nuclear shielding
Melanosomes transferred to keratinocytes can localize above the nucleus (“supranuclear caps”), reducing UV reaching nuclear DNA. This is a geometric “umbrella” effect independent of chemistry.

#### Melanosome architecture
Protection depends not only on total melanin, but on:
- **melanosome size and number**
- **distribution** within keratinocytes (dispersed vs aggregated)
- **persistence** through epidermal layers (how far pigment remains as keratinocytes differentiate)
- **eu:pheo ratio** and melanin chemical state

These factors change:
- how much UV reaches basal proliferative layers,
- how much UVA-driven oxidative stress occurs in deeper compartments,
- and how much damage is concentrated near nuclear DNA.

#### Chemical buffering
- **ROS quenching**: melanin can quench some reactive species and excited states.
- **Metal chelation**: binding Fe/Cu can reduce free-metal catalysis of radical reactions, but can also become pro-oxidant in specific redox contexts (see below).

#### Signaling and repair modulation
Pigmentation is coupled to signaling systems (notably MC1R/cAMP axis) that can alter:
- DNA repair capacity,
- antioxidant enzyme programs,
- inflammatory responses.

#### Induced pigmentation and protection
Induced tanning increases melanin and can reduce sunburn sensitivity, but:
- protection is generally **modest** compared with constitutive high eumelanin states,
- protection can be uneven (depends on distribution and exposure history),
- and the process of acquiring a tan often requires UV exposure that can itself generate damage.

### Eumelanin vs pheomelanin
- **Eumelanin** (brown-black):
  - typically more photostable,
  - stronger UV attenuation,
  - better ROS quenching and metal-chelation behavior (context-dependent).
- **Pheomelanin** (red-yellow, sulfur-containing):
  - more photounstable,
  - more prone to pro-oxidant behavior under UVA in some contexts,
  - linked to higher UV sensitivity phenotypes (often via MC1R biology).

Structural note (important for interpreting “melanin chemistry” claims): melanin is not a single molecule with a canonical, sequence-defined chemical structure. Biological melanins are heterogeneous ensembles of irregular oligomers and aggregates whose detailed bonding patterns, oxidation states, hydration, and metal binding vary with synthesis conditions and tissue context. In practice, “what melanin is” is often operationally defined by precursor pathway (eumelanin-leaning vs pheomelanin-leaning) plus measurable markers rather than by a single resolved polymer structure. For eumelanin structural models and supramolecular buildup concepts: [The Supramolecular Buildup of Eumelanin: Structures, Mechanisms, Controllability (Int J Mol Sci, 2017; full text PDF)](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/ijms/ijms-18-01901/article_deploy/ijms-18-01901.pdf?version=1504680821). For a chemical-analysis entry point (marker-based quantification): [Ito et al., AHPO melanin analysis methods (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21535429/).

### Melanogenesis
Core pathway (simplified):
- Tyrosine → L-DOPA → DOPAquinone (tyrosinase)
- Branch:
  - low cysteine → eumelanin route (DHI/DHICA polymerization)
  - high cysteine → pheomelanin route (cysteinyldopa → benzothiazine derivatives)

Redox relevance:
- melanogenesis consumes reducing equivalents and interacts with glutathione pools,
- the balance of eu/pheo can shift baseline oxidative stress and responses to UVA/UVB.
In shorthand:
Tyrosine → L-DOPA → DOPAquinone, then low cysteine favors eumelanin polymerization (DHI/DHICA route) while high cysteine favors pheomelanin (cysteinyldopa → benzothiazine derivatives).

### Genetic control: MC1R axis as a switch
MC1R signaling (via α-MSH / ACTH) tends to favor eumelanin and can also influence:
- antioxidant responses,
  - DNA repair responses,
- inflammatory tone in melanocyte lineage context.

Loss-of-function MC1R variants (common in red-hair/fair-skin phenotypes) shift the system toward pheomelanin and altered repair/redox dynamics.

### Spatial photoprotection: melanosomes as nuclear shields
Melanosomes transferred to keratinocytes can form supranuclear caps that reduce UV reaching nuclear DNA. This is a geometrical protection mechanism layered on top of chemistry.

### Delayed damage concepts: “dark CPDs” / chemiexcitation
Some mechanistic models propose that UV exposure can initiate chemical pathways that continue producing DNA lesions after irradiation stops, via excited intermediates generated in melanogenesis/redox chemistry (often discussed in relation to reactive nitrogen/oxygen species chemistry).

If true, this changes the naive exposure model:
- not only “damage during exposure,” but “damage during recovery window,” potentially modulated by:
  - melanin chemistry,
  - ROS/NO balance,
  - antioxidant availability,
  - inflammation state.

### Melanin, metals, and the redox switch
Melanin binds metals, notably iron and copper. This can be protective by reducing free metal-catalyzed radical formation, but under some conditions it can create pro-oxidant microenvironments via Fenton-like chemistry when antioxidants are depleted.

### Pigment and tumor therapy response
Pigment in melanoma can plausibly alter:
- radiative absorption and local heating,
- oxidative stress balance,
- and drug binding/partitioning.
These are mechanistic reasons why pigment state could interact with therapy outcomes, independent of UV exposure per se.

### Melanin outside skin
Melanin-like pigments exist in:
- **eye** (RPE, uveal tract),
- **brain** (neuromelanin),
- **inner ear** (melanocyte-like populations).

These pigments often show roles in:
- light absorption (eye),
- redox/metal binding (eye/brain),
- tissue-specific protective functions.

But “melanin exists outside skin” does not imply a single universal role in cancer prevention; these tissues have distinct microenvironments and distinct cancer etiologies, for example uveal versus cutaneous melanoma.

## Vitamin D biology

### Synthesis and metabolism
- UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D3 in skin → vitamin D3.
- Vitamin D3 is hydroxylated:
  - in liver → **25(OH)D** (status marker),
  - in kidney and other tissues → **1,25(OH)₂D** (active hormone).

### Forms and measurement
Common entities:
- **Vitamin D2 vs D3** (inputs)
- **25(OH)D** (circulating status; what most labs report)
- **1,25(OH)₂D** (active; tightly regulated; not a simple “more is better” marker)

Vitamin D’s relevance to cancer is often argued via:
- VDR-mediated regulation of differentiation, apoptosis, and immune signaling.

### Intracrine vitamin D signaling
Some tissues and immune cells express enzymes that locally convert 25(OH)D to active forms, enabling local (intracrine/paracrine) signaling that may not be captured by simple “serum 25(OH)D only” reasoning.
In shorthand:
UVB in skin → vitamin D3 → liver 25-hydroxylation (25-hydroxyvitamin D) → kidney and local tissue activation (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D) → vitamin D receptor (VDR) transcriptional programs.

### Vitamin D and immune regulation

Immune cells express VDR and can respond to vitamin D signaling. Mechanistic themes often discussed include:
- **Antigen presentation tuning**: shifts in dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation state.
- **T cell polarization**: changes in balance among inflammatory vs regulatory programs (context-dependent).
- **Innate effector programs**: modulation of antimicrobial peptides and barrier immunity pathways.

This is relevant to cancer because tumor control is often an immune surveillance problem:
- if vitamin D signaling improves effective surveillance, it could be protective;
- if it pushes toward a more tolerogenic immune state in a given context, effects could differ by tumor type and immune baseline.

#### More detail: immune cell programs often implicated
Mechanistic discussions commonly include (direction can be context-dependent):
- **Dendritic cells / APCs**: altered maturation state, co-stimulation, cytokine profile.
- **CD4 T cell programs**: shifts among Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg-associated programs.
- **CD8 T cell state**: potential effects on activation vs exhaustion balance through cytokine environment.
- **B cells**: effects on antibody and regulatory programs in some models.
- **Innate immunity**: induction of antimicrobial peptides, for example cathelicidin pathways, and barrier immune tone.

The key point for cancer is not “vitamin D boosts immunity” in a generic way; it is that vitamin D can plausibly reweight immune programs that may help or hinder tumor control depending on tumor immunogenicity and baseline inflammation.

In shorthand:
25-hydroxyvitamin D availability + local activation in immune cells → VDR signaling → shifts in antigen presentation and T cell programs → changes in tumor surveillance pressure and inflammatory set points.

### Vitamin D and tumor cell programs
In vitro and animal literature frequently cites VDR-linked regulation of:
- differentiation programs,
- cell-cycle checkpoints,
- apoptosis susceptibility,
- and sometimes angiogenesis-related signaling.

Whether these mechanisms dominate in humans depends on tissue context, dosing, baseline deficiency, and duration.

### Why observational, randomized trial, and Mendelian randomization results can diverge
Even without leaning on any single study, divergence is plausible because:
- observational 25(OH)D is a composite of sun exposure, diet, adiposity, inflammation, and behavior,
- supplementation changes 25(OH)D without recreating UVA-driven pathways,
- genetic instruments represent lifelong differences and assume specific causal structures.

Mechanistically: UV → (vitamin D + non–vitamin D pathways). A vitamin D pill probes only part of that vector.

## Systemic mediators beyond vitamin D

Several pathways are frequently proposed to explain associations between sun exposure and non-skin outcomes:
- **nitric oxide (NO)-related pathways**: UVA can mobilize NO-related species stored in skin, potentially affecting vascular tone.
- **neuroendocrine signaling**: skin is an active endocrine/immune organ; UV can modulate peptides and stress-axis signaling.
- **circadian/behavioral coupling**: daylight exposure shifts sleep timing, physical activity, and metabolic rhythms, which can secondarily impact cancer-relevant physiology.

This category is important because it provides a mechanistic rationale for:
- possible non–vitamin D benefits of sunlight,
- and for why vitamin D supplementation may fail to reproduce them.

### Nitric oxide photobiology
UVA can mobilize NO-related species in skin:
- photolysis of nitrite/nitrate-related pools and nitrosothiols is often proposed,
- downstream effects would be vascular tone and signaling shifts.

The mechanistic relevance to cancer endpoints, if any, would likely be indirect (via cardiovascular health, immune modulation, or systemic inflammation), and is therefore highly sensitive to confounding in observational mortality studies.

### Neuroendocrine / cutaneous neuroimmune signaling
Skin is an active signaling organ. UV can influence local production of mediators (peptides, cytokines) that can have systemic effects.

Other routes that can look like “UV effects” in cancer data are indirect but still mechanistic:

- Daylight exposure co-varies with physical activity, metabolic health, sleep timing, and psychosocial patterns. These can change inflammation and immune function and can therefore change cancer-relevant physiology.
- Pigmentation alters UV penetration and vitamin D synthesis efficiency. Pigmentation-linked genetics can also shift melanogenesis chemistry, redox biology, and immune signaling, so pigmentation can act as both a modifier and a confounder.
- Many cancers are shaped by immune history and chronic infection and inflammation. If UV exposure measurably shifts immune programs, it can interact with infection control, chronic inflammatory tone, and immune exhaustion states.

## Route 5 details: UVA and visible to melanin to electrophysiology

UVA and visible light can couple into electrophysiology through melanin if melanin behaves as a transducer between photons and charge and hydration state.

Core chain:
UVA and visible dose perturbs melanin charge, proton, and redox state as a hydrated mixed ionic and electronic material. That can change local ion activity and pH near membranes and modulate Vmem and mitochondrial ΔΨm.

The *direction* of any downstream effect (toward more proliferation vs more differentiation / growth arrest) is **not implied by the chain**; it is an empirical sign question.

This is speculative, but it can be expressed as concrete mechanisms with falsifiable predictions.

### Why melanin is a plausible transducer candidate
If one is looking for a molecule/structure that can couple photons to cell-scale electrophysiology without invoking direct UV access to internal organs, melanin stands out because it combines several properties that rarely co-occur:

- **Broadband absorption (UV → visible → near-IR)**: many biological chromophores are narrow-band; melanin is an unusually broadband sink for photons.
- **Persistent radical / redox-buffer behavior**: melanin can host stable radical character and participate in redox chemistry, creating a plausible route from photon absorption to changes in electron/proton availability.
- **Hydration-dependent charge transport (mixed ionic/electronic claims)**: in “semiconductor-like” framings, melanin is treated as a hydrated, disordered polymer whose conductivity and charge storage depend strongly on water content and protonation.
- **Large surface area in granules (melanosomes)**: packaging into organelles creates mesoscale interfaces where surface charge, pH microdomains, and ion activity effects could matter even if the bulk cytosol is well-buffered.
- **Geometry/localization**: melanosomes can be perinuclear (UV protection) and/or distributed; if the effect is real, spatial arrangement creates testable differences in electrical coupling and field effects.

None of these imply the hypothesis is true; they explain why “melanin as transducer” is not interchangeable with “any chromophore produces ROS.”

### Coupling chain: photon absorption → melanosome charge and proton dynamics → membrane potentials
This chain treats melanin as a light-driven perturbation to charge and proton handling that can propagate into membrane potential \(V_m\), intracellular pH \(pH_i\), and mitochondrial membrane potential \(\Delta\Psi_m\).

#### What water structure means here
In this section, “water structure” refers to **measurable physical properties of water near surfaces** (especially charged polymer surfaces) that change ionic/electrical behavior:
- **Interfacial/bound water fraction**: how much water is strongly associated with macromolecular surfaces versus “bulk-like.”
- **Effective dielectric environment** near surfaces (affects electrostatic screening and protein conformations).
- **Proton mobility and buffering** near interfaces, for example Grotthuss-style proton hopping and local proton availability.
- **Ion activity vs concentration**: channels and enzymes respond to local activity; interfacial environments can change activity coefficients.
- **Local electrostatic potentials** near surfaces (surface potential / Donnan-like effects), which bias ion distributions on nanometer–micron scales.

The claim is not that “water becomes a new substance.” It is that **interfacial physics can change effective ion/proton behavior enough to matter for channels/pumps**.

#### Step 1: UV, UVA, and visible as the relevant input
- In skin, **UVA** penetrates deeper than UVB and can deliver meaningful photon flux to basal layers and dermal-adjacent compartments.
- Melanin absorbs strongly across UVA and visible wavelengths; therefore UVA/visible are the most plausible spectral regions for “melanin transduction” effects that are not simply UVB photoproduct biology.

This framing predicts **action-spectrum specificity**: effects (if real) should track melanin absorption rather than erythema weighting.

#### Step 2: what photon absorption changes in melanin
The claim is not “melanin makes ROS.” The specific claim is that illumination shifts melanin’s **charge/proton/redox state** in ways that are relevant on cellular timescales:
- **Excited-state and photoredox shifts**: absorbed photons can change the distribution of redox states and radical character within the polymer.
- **Proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET)-style behavior** (as proposed in some models): changes in electronic state can couple to protonation state and vice versa, making local pH and hydration mechanically relevant.
- **Charge storage / capacitance-like behavior**: a highly hydrated, disordered polymer surface can, in principle, store charge and alter local electric fields at interfaces.
- **Hydration dependence**: because water content and proton mobility can dominate charge transport in hydrated polymers, “water structure” enters as a boundary-condition on melanin’s effective conductivity and surface potential, not as a separate mystical entity.

#### Step 3: how that could reweight ion activity and pH near membranes
Even if bulk cytosolic concentrations remain stable, biology often depends on **ion activity and microdomains** near charged surfaces:
- **Surface charge and local electric fields** can change the near-membrane distribution of ions (Debye-layer scale), affecting effective concentrations seen by channels and transporters.
- **Local pH microdomains (pHi near membranes/organelles)** can shift channel gating and transporter directionality (many channels/transporters are pH-sensitive).
- **Melanosome membranes themselves** maintain gradients with an acidic lumen via pumps and exchangers, including V-type H⁺-ATPase plus counter-ion conductances. If melanin illumination changes proton availability or buffering at or near the melanosome membrane, it can couple to local proton fluxes and local pHi.

Missing intermediate steps (what has to happen physically):
- **Step 3a (interface → water physics)**: illumination shifts melanin surface potential/protonation → changes interfacial water polarization, effective dielectric screening, and proton mobility near the melanosome surface.
- **Step 3b (water physics → ion activity)**: altered interfacial conditions shift local activity coefficients and electrostatic potentials → change near-surface ion activities (K⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺, H⁺) even if bulk concentrations barely change.
- **Step 3c (ion activity → protein state)**: because many membrane proteins sense local pH/ionic strength/surface potential, those changes can alter transporter directionality, channel gating thresholds, and protein conformations/trafficking.

Necessary conditions for Step 3 to matter:
- the effect must be large enough and long-lived enough to outcompete cytosolic buffering/diffusion,
- the altered region must couple to membrane-adjacent environments (directly or via global modulators like pHi/Ca²⁺),
- and the “port” must be a rate-limiting controller (a channel/pump that actually sets Vmem/pHi/ΔΨm).

#### Step 4: coupling into plasma membrane Vmem via ion channels and pumps
Vmem is not a vague “field”; it is the consequence of:
- **selective permeabilities** (channels and transporters) for K⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺,
- **electrogenic pumps**, for example Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase,
- and **gradients** established by metabolism and transport.

At a coarse level, the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) picture says Vmem depends on relative permeabilities and ionic activities. Therefore, if a melanin-driven process changes what channels “see” locally (pH microdomains, surface charge, local ion activity), it can bias Vmem even without changing bulk ion concentrations.

Two additional constraints make this mechanistically sharp:
- **Local-to-global coupling**: to change whole-cell Vmem, the perturbation must either occur near the plasma membrane so it affects the channels that set Vmem, or it must change the state of those channels and pumps globally via pHi and Ca²⁺ dependent modulation of conductances or trafficking. A purely perinuclear microdomain that never couples to membrane conductances cannot shift Vmem.
- **Directionality**: a “depolarizing” shift typically corresponds to **increased inward Na⁺/Ca²⁺ conductance**, **decreased outward K⁺ conductance**, altered **Cl⁻** handling, and/or changes in electrogenic pump currents. Therefore, a melanin-centered story must ultimately identify which conductance(s) change and why.

Plausible coupling routes (none are proven; each is testable):
- **pHi and proton flux effects**: many channels and transporters are pH-sensitive. If illumination changes proton buffering/availability near melanosomes and these microdomains communicate with membrane-adjacent pH, channel gating and exchanger directionality can shift.
- **Surface charge / near-membrane ion activity**: charged surfaces alter local ion distributions on nanometer scales. Channel pores and voltage sensors operate in that near-field regime; shifts in effective local ionic strength and surface potential can alter gating thresholds.
- **Pump load and transporter coupling**: pHi shifts change the load on Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers, HCO₃⁻ transporters, and proton pumps; those changes feed back into Na⁺ gradients and therefore into Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase currents and secondary transport.
- **Ca²⁺ entry as an amplifier**: small Vmem shifts can change Ca²⁺ entry (through voltage-dependent mechanisms where present, or through indirect effects on driving force), and Ca²⁺ can strongly reprogram electrophysiology via Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels and other conductances.

#### Step 5: coupling into mitochondria and the proton motive force
Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is generated by the electron transport chain pumping protons to create a proton motive force. ΔΨm is sensitive to:
- cytosolic **ADP/ATP** (demand),
- **Ca²⁺** (mitochondrial uptake can activate dehydrogenases and change electron flow),
- **pHi and buffering** (proton availability and transporter equilibria),
- and **redox state** insofar as it changes electron supply and leak.

Therefore, if a light→melanin process measurably alters any combination of pHi microdomains, Ca²⁺ handling, or membrane conductances, it can plausibly shift ΔΨm and downstream metabolic phenotype without invoking “water splitting.”

#### Step 6: from Vmem and ΔΨm to proliferative state
In bioelectric control frameworks, Vmem is treated as a **state variable** that correlates with (and in some systems can help control) proliferation vs differentiation:
- Many proliferative / embryonic-like states are **more depolarized** than mature differentiated states.
- Many cancers show **ion channel remodeling** (K⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺ transport changes), altered pHi regulation, and altered ΔΨm dynamics.

This is not a claim that “all cancer = depolarization” or that depolarization is sufficient. It is a claim that **persistent shifts in electrical set points** can change Ca²⁺ dynamics, transporter driving forces, and checkpoint thresholds in ways that can bias phenotype.

#### Direction is not inferred by the mechanism chain
From “UV/UVA perturbs melanin” you can get at least three qualitatively different outcomes:
- **No net effect**: the perturbation is too local, too buffered, or too transient to change the dominant plasma-membrane conductances that set Vmem.
- **Pro-proliferative sign (often framed as depolarizing)**: a sustained shift toward depolarized set points increases permissive Ca²⁺ signaling, supports higher pHi, and favors proliferation/de-differentiation programs.
- **Anti-proliferative sign (hyperpolarizing or stress-triggering)**: the same upstream perturbation could increase K⁺ conductance or pump currents, stabilize hyperpolarized set points, and bias differentiation; or it could trigger mitochondrial stress responses that reduce proliferation.

In other words, the chain specifies *where* to look (ports A–D), not the sign of the final arrow. The sign is determined by which conductances change, in which direction, and on what timescale.
One way to keep the full chain in view:
UVA/visible dose → melanin charge/proton state → interfacial electrostatics and pH microdomains → channel/pump state and currents → \(V_m\) and Ca²⁺ handling → \(\Delta\Psi_m\) and metabolic set points → proliferation/differentiation bias → cancer-like phenotypes.

#### Quantitative scaffold: what would connect UVA and visible to ΔVmem
To make “UVA/visible → melanin → water/interface → Vmem” quantitative, you need a mapping from **photon absorption** to **a change in membrane currents**.

**1) Photon input at depth**
Let `E_lambda` be spectral irradiance and `I_lambda(z)` the irradiance at depth `z`.

$$
I_{\lambda}(z) \approx I_{\lambda,0}\,\exp\big(-\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda)\,z\big)
$$

$$
P_{\mathrm{abs}}^{\mathrm{mel}}(z) \approx \int I_{\lambda}(z)\,\mu_{a}^{\mathrm{mel}}(\lambda)\,d\lambda
$$

$$
\dot{n}_{\mathrm{abs}}(z) \approx \int \frac{I_{\lambda}(z)\,\mu_{a}^{\mathrm{mel}}(\lambda)}{hc/\lambda}\,d\lambda
$$

**2) Absorption → melanin charge/proton perturbation (the key unknown)**
The theory needs an effective yield mapping absorbed photons to a surface/charge variable:

$$
\dot{\sigma} = q\,\phi\,\dot{n}_{\mathrm{abs}} - \frac{\sigma}{\tau}
$$

where `sigma` is an effective surface charge density (or an equivalent interfacial state variable), `phi` is “charges (or protons) moved per absorbed photon,” and `tau` is a relaxation time. If `phi` is tiny or `tau` is very short, the downstream effect will be negligible.

**3) Surface charge → interfacial potential (screened electrostatics)**
Any electric-field coupling is strongly limited by screening. The characteristic screening length in electrolyte is the Debye length:

$$
\lambda_D \sim \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\varepsilon_0 kT}{2 N_A e^2 I}}
$$

where `I` is ionic strength. A key implication is that long-range electrostatic influence decays rapidly; the mechanism must work through **near-surface microenvironments** or through **global modulators** (pHi/Ca²⁺/trafficking) rather than expecting a large field to propagate across the cell.

If you model the melanin interface as a charged surface, a Gouy–Chapman-style relation links `sigma` to a surface potential `psi0` (nonlinear in general). The useful point is that the same `Delta sigma` produces different `Delta psi0` depending on ionic strength and dielectric environment.

**4) Interfacial potential / pHi → changes in channel/pump behavior**
At the level of electrophysiology, you can treat this as a perturbation to conductances and/or reversal potentials:

$$
I_i \approx G_i(\psi_0,\mathrm{pH}_i,\mathrm{Ca})\,(V_m - E_i)
$$

$$
\Delta I_i \approx \frac{\partial I_i}{\partial \psi_0}\,\Delta\psi_0 + \frac{\partial I_i}{\partial \mathrm{pH}_i}\,\Delta \mathrm{pH}_i + \cdots
$$

**5) Current balance → ΔVmem**
At steady state the membrane voltage satisfies current balance:

$$
\sum_i I_i(V_m) = 0
$$

$$
\Delta V_m \approx - \frac{\sum_i \Delta I_i}{\sum_i \left(\partial I_i/\partial V_m\right)}
$$

This makes the missing inference explicit: a UV-driven interface effect changes `V_m` only if it produces a nontrivial `sum_i Delta I_i` through identifiable channels/pumps.

**Quantitative falsifiers / constraints**
- **Ionic strength dependence**: if the effect is electrostatic/interfacial, the magnitude should change with ionic strength/buffering conditions in predictable ways; if it does not, the proposed coupling route is likely wrong.
- **Action spectrum**: `Delta V_m` should track melanin-weighted absorption (UVA/visible) rather than UVB CPD-weighted dose.
- **Timescale**: `tau` must be long enough that `sigma`, `psi0`, or `pHi` perturbations can couple into channel/pump state changes rather than averaging to zero.
